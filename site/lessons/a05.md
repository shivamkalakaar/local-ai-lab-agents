# Why train-time scaling?

## From answer time to training time
- a01–a04 spent more compute at answer time (sampling, verifiers, search). Here we spend it at training time.
- Loop: the model tries, a checker grades, we train on what worked.
- Verifiable reward: a reward computed by a program (exact match, unit tests), not a person or a judge model.
  - Cheap, and hard to fool
- Three readings, three steps forward:
  - STaR (2022): keep the right answers, fine-tune on them
  - DeepSeekMath (2024): GRPO, RL without a critic model
  - DAPO (2025): four fixes that make GRPO work for long reasoning

## The three papers side by side
| | STaR | DeepSeekMath (GRPO) | DAPO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model | GPT-J 6B | DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B | Qwen2.5-32B base |
| Learning | Supervised fine-tuning on kept rationales | RL, group-relative advantage | RL, GRPO + 4 fixes |
| Wrong answers | Ignored (or rationalized with a hint) | Pushed down (negative advantage) | Pushed down; all-wrong groups dropped |
| Reward | Answer matches the label | Trained reward model | Rule: +1 right, −1 wrong |
| Headline | CQA 72.5% | MATH 51.7% | AIME 2024: 50 |

## Key terms
- Policy: the model being trained; it "picks" each next token.
- Rollout: one sampled answer from the policy.
- Advantage: how much better one answer did than expected. Positive → do more of this; negative → do less.
- Reward model: a learned model that scores answers. A rule-based reward is a program instead.
- Pass@K: right if any of K samples is right. Maj@K: right if the majority vote of K samples is right.
- Entropy: how spread out the model's next-token choices are. Low entropy = it always says the same thing.

# STaR: Self-Taught Reasoner (Zelikman, Wu, Mu & Goodman, 2022)

## The problem STaR solves
- Rationale: step-by-step reasoning written before the final answer ("chain of thought").
- Rationales help on math and commonsense questions. Two ways to get them, both flawed:
  - Build a fine-tuning set of rationales by hand or by template: expensive, or only works when a general solution is known
  - Few-shot prompting with a few rationale examples: cheap, but well below a model fine-tuned to answer directly
- STaR: start from a few examples (e.g. 10) plus a big dataset of questions and answers with no rationales. Let the model write the rationales.

## The STaR loop (Algorithm 1)
- 1. Prompt the model with a few rationale examples; generate a rationale and answer for every training question.
- 2. Filter: keep only rationales whose answer matches the label.
- 3. Rationalize: for each wrong one, give the correct answer as a hint and generate again.
- 4. Keep rationalized rationales that now reach the right answer; store them without the hint.
- 5. Fine-tune the original model M on everything kept.
- 6. Repeat with the newly trained model generating, until performance plateaus.

## Why retrain from the original model
- Each outer loop fine-tunes the original pre-trained model M, not the last fine-tuned one.
  - The paper does this "to avoid overfitting"
- Training steps grow each loop: 40 steps in the first outer loop, then +20% per loop.
- 100-step learning-rate warmup, then a constant rate (GPT-J repo defaults).
- "Training more slowly at the beginning ultimately benefits model performance."

## Rationalization: reason backwards from the answer
| Rationale generation | Rationalization |
|---|---|
| Prompt: question only | Prompt: question + "(b) grocery cart (CORRECT)" |
| Model may fail and get no training signal | Model reasons backwards to the given answer |
| Kept if the answer is right | Kept if it reaches the answer; stored **as if no hint was given** |

- Without it, the loop stalls: it "receives no direct training signal for problems it fails to solve."
- Benefit 1: exposes the model to hard problems it would never solve alone.
- Benefit 2: a larger training set.

## STaR as policy gradient (Equations 1–2)
- Objective: J = Σ over questions of the expected value of 1(ŷ = y).
  - 1(ŷ = y): reward 1 if the predicted answer equals the label, else 0
- Gradient: ∇J = Σ E[ 1(ŷ = y) · ∇ log p(ŷ, r̂ | x) ].
  - ∇ log p: the direction that makes this rationale and answer more likely
  - Multiplying by 1(ŷ = y) throws away every wrong sample: that is the filter
- STaR approximates this two ways: greedy decoding (less variance, more biased exploration) and several gradient steps on the same batch.
- Rationalization = sampling from p(r | x, y), a better search space; an "off-policy" estimate.

## Datasets used

## Results: arithmetic (Figure 4)
- After 16 STaR iterations: 89.5% overall accuracy.
- Baseline trained on 10,000 examples without rationales for 5,000 steps: 76.3%.

## Are the rationales any good?
- Human study: 20 crowdworkers each ranked rationales for 10 CQA questions both models got right.
- STaR rationales were ranked above few-shot ones 30% more often (p = .039).
- STaR rationales were preferred over human-written ones 74% more often (p < .001).
  - The authors say this shows how hard good rationales are to collect, not human-level reasoning
- Failure cases: statements on topic but not arguments; "the question implies the answer"; talking about one specific person instead of a general rule.

## STaR ablations: what mattered
- Few-shot prompts kept during fine-tuning: 60.9% → 68.8% without rationalization; 69.9% → 72.5% with it.
- Few-shot prompts also reduce "drift": later rationales stay closer to the original style.
- Higher sampling temperature instead of rationalization (0.5 or 0.7): consistently worse.
  - More right answers with wrong reasoning; arithmetic scratchpads "diverge into meaninglessness"
  - Also slower: 10 samples ≈ 10x the generation time

## STaR limitations (stated)
- The first loop only works if few-shot accuracy is above chance. GPT-2 could not bootstrap even on arithmetic.
- High-chance tasks (e.g. yes/no) produce many right answers with bad rationales. Filtering those is an open problem.
- Writing a good hint is not always easy for a new task.
- CQA itself has biases, typos and ambiguous questions.

# DeepSeekMath, part 1: the data and the base model (Shao et al., 2024)

## Why this paper matters here
- Two contributions:
  - A 120B-token math corpus from Common Crawl, giving a strong 7B base model
  - GRPO, a cheaper PPO for the RL stage
- Pipeline: DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B → continued pre-training (DeepSeekMath-Base) → instruction tuning (-Instruct) → GRPO (-RL).
- Headline: 51.7% on MATH without tools or voting; 60.9% with self-consistency over 64 samples (abstract).

## Building the math corpus (Figure 2)
- Seed: OpenWebMath. Train a fastText classifier on 500,000 seed pages (positive) and 500,000 Common Crawl pages (negative).
  - fastText: a fast, simple text classifier
- Deduplicate Common Crawl to 40B HTML pages; keep the top-scoring math pages.
- Find math domains: any domain with over 10% of pages collected (e.g. mathoverflow.net). Humans mark math URL paths; add them to the seed.
- Four iterations → 35.5M pages, 120B tokens. In round 4, ~98% was already collected in round 3, so they stopped.
- Decontamination: drop any text sharing a 10-gram with GSM8K, MATH, CMATH or AGIEval.

- Trained 500B tokens: 56% math corpus, 4% AlgebraicStack, 10% arXiv, 20% GitHub code, 10% natural language.

- Code first, then math beats general text first, then math (Table 6, 1.3B model):
  - Code → math: GSM8K 21.9%, MATH 15.3%

- With tools it approaches 60% on MATH (57.4%).

# DeepSeekMath, part 2: GRPO

## PPO, the starting point (Equation 1)
- PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization): the standard RL method for fine-tuning LLMs.
- Objective, per token: min( ratio · A, clip(ratio, 1−ε, 1+ε) · A ), averaged over the answer's tokens.
  - ratio = π_θ(token) / π_old(token): new probability over the probability when the answer was sampled
  - A: advantage, from GAE (generalized advantage estimation) using a learned value function
  - clip(…, 1−ε, 1+ε): stop any one update from moving a token's probability too far
- KL penalty in the reward (Equation 2): r_t = reward − β · log(π_θ / π_ref).
  - π_ref: the starting SFT model; β: how hard to stay close to it

## The problem with PPO's critic
- The value function (critic) is usually another model as big as the policy.
  - Big memory and compute cost
- LLM rewards come only at the last token, so a per-token value is hard to learn.
- GRPO's idea: drop the critic. Sample several answers to the same question; use their average reward as the baseline.

## PPO vs GRPO (Figure 4)
| PPO | GRPO |
|---|---|
| Policy, reference, reward and **value** models | Policy, reference and reward models; no value model |
| A second network (critic) guesses the expected reward | The group's mean reward is the expected reward |
| Advantage per token from GAE | Advantage from the group's rewards |
| KL penalty added to the reward | KL added straight to the loss |
| One answer per prompt is enough | Needs a group of answers per prompt |

## The GRPO objective (Equation 3)
- For each question q, sample G answers o_1 … o_G from the old policy.
- J = average over the G answers of [ average over each answer's tokens of min( ratio · Â, clip(ratio, 1−ε, 1+ε) · Â ) − β · KL(π_θ ‖ π_ref) ].
  - Â_i,t: advantage of token t in answer i, from the group only
  - 1/G and 1/|o_i|: average per answer first, then across answers (DAPO changes this)
  - β · KL: keep the model close to the reference model
- Group-relative scoring fits reward models, which are trained on comparisons between answers to the same question.

## The KL estimator (Equation 4)
- KL = π_ref/π_θ − log(π_ref/π_θ) − 1, per token.
  - π_ref/π_θ: how much more the reference model likes this token than the current model
  - Always positive; zero only when the two models agree
- Unbiased estimator (Schulman, 2020).
- Put in the loss, not the reward, so it does not complicate the advantage.

## Outcome vs process supervision (Sections 4.1.2–4.1.3)
| Outcome supervision (OS) | Process supervision (PS) |
|---|---|
| One reward at the end of each answer | One reward at the end of each reasoning step |
| Â = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r), same for every token | Normalize all step rewards by their mean and std |
| | Token advantage = sum of normalized rewards of the steps after it |

- Process rewards come from a process reward model (scores each step).

## Iterative GRPO (Algorithm 1, Section 4.1.4)
- Problem: as the policy improves, the old reward model can no longer judge it well.
- Each iteration:
  - Set the reference model to the current policy
  - Train the policy for M steps with GRPO
  - Train the reward model on new samples from the policy, replaying 10% old data
- Figure 6: two rounds of iteration; the first gives the biggest gain.

## GRPO training setup (Section 4.2)
- Start: DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B.
- Data: ~144K chain-of-thought questions about GSM8K and MATH from the SFT set. Other SFT questions held out to test transfer.
- Reward: a reward model trained from DeepSeekMath-Base 7B (learning rate 2e-5).
- Policy learning rate 1e-6. KL coefficient 0.04.
- 64 samples per question. Max length 1024. Batch size 1024.
- One policy update per exploration stage.

## One view of SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO (Equation 5, Table 10)
- Every method's gradient = data source × gradient coefficient × ∇ log π(token).
  - Data source: where the training answers come from
  - Reward function: rule (answer correct?) or model (reward model)
  - Gradient coefficient: how hard to push each answer up or down

| Method | Data source | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| SFT | Human-chosen answers | – |
| RFT | Samples from the SFT model (offline) | Rule |
| DPO | Pairs sampled from the SFT model | Rule |
| Online RFT | Samples from the current policy | Rule |
| PPO / GRPO | Samples from the current policy | Model |

## What the comparisons show (Figure 5, 1.3B model)
- Online beats offline: Online RFT ≈ RFT early, then pulls clearly ahead.
  - Later on, the policy differs from the SFT model, so fresh samples matter more
- GRPO beats Online RFT: GRPO pushes wrong answers down and scales by reward; RFT only pushes right answers up, all equally.
- GRPO+PS beats GRPO+OS: step-level signals help.

## Why RL works: Maj@K vs Pass@K (Figure 7)
- Instruct vs RL model, GSM8K and MATH, K from 1 to 64, temperature 0.7.
- Maj@K goes up after RL. Pass@K does not.
- Meaning: RL makes the right answer that was already in the top K come out more often.
  - "Rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities"
- The authors blame partly their data: only SFT questions and plain nucleus sampling.

## Future directions and limitations (Sections 5.2.3, 6)
- Data: out-of-distribution questions, tree-search sampling, faster inference.
- Algorithms: all methods fully trust the reward. Even PRM800K has ~20% wrong labels. Need RL robust to noisy rewards.
- Reward models: generalize better, show uncertainty, cheaper process reward models.
- Limits: weaker on geometry and theorem proving (fails on triangles and ellipses); few-shot examples don't help it as they help GPT-4.

# GRPO by hand

## GRPO on a contract question
| Rewards for 4 answers | Mean | Advantages | Teaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| [1, 1, 1, 0] | 0.75 | Right answers +, wrong one − | Shift toward the reasoning that worked |
| [1, 1, 1, 1] | 1.0 | All zero | Nothing |

- msa_20, 7.1: "...SHALL NOT EXCEED THE FEES PAID BY CUSTOMER IN THE THIRTY-SIX (36) MONTHS PRECEDING THE CLAIM."
- Question: "Is the liability cap at most 12 months of fees? End with Answer: yes or no." (Right answer: no.)
- A program reads the last line and gives 1 if right.

## GRPO advantages in code
```python
def grpo_advantages(r, eps=1e-4):
    r = np.asarray(r, dtype=float)
    return (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + eps)
```
- `r`: the rewards for one group; `eps`: a tiny number so we never divide by zero.
- Line 2: turn the list into an array of decimals.
- Line 3: subtract the group mean, divide by the spread. Grading on a curve.
- Advantages in a group always add up to about 0: the notebook checks this.

# DAPO (Yu et al., ByteDance Seed and Tsinghua AIR, 2025)

## The problem DAPO solves
- o1 and DeepSeek-R1 showed RL can teach long reasoning, but hid key training details.
- The team's first naive GRPO run on Qwen2.5-32B: 30 points on AIME 2024. DeepSeek's RL got 47.
- Causes found: entropy collapse, reward noise, unstable training.
- DAPO: Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization. Fully open: algorithm, code (built on verl) and data.
- Result: 50 on AIME 2024 with 50% of DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B's training steps (Figure 1).

## Two simplifications before the fixes
- Remove the KL penalty (Section 2.3).
  - In chat tuning (RLHF), staying close to the start model is the goal
  - For long reasoning, the model should move far from where it started
- Rule-based reward (Equation 7): R = 1 if the answer is equivalent to the label, else −1.
  - Learned reward models tend to get hacked; a rule does not

## Fix 1: Clip-Higher (Section 3.1)
- Entropy collapse: entropy drops fast; answers in a group become nearly identical (Figure 2b).
- Why: the upper clip limits rare tokens far more than common ones. With ε = 0.2:

| Old probability | Max new probability (×1.2) |
|---|---|
| 0.01 (an "exploration" token) | 0.012 |
| 0.9 (an "exploitation" token) | 1.08 (no real limit) |

- Tokens hitting the upper clip have mean probability below 0.2 (Figure 3a).
- Fix: decouple the bounds. ε_low = 0.2, ε_high = 0.28.
  - ε_low not raised: that would push rare tokens to 0 and collapse sampling

## Fix 2: Dynamic Sampling (Section 3.2)
- If all G answers are right (or all wrong), every advantage is 0 → zero gradient.
- The share of all-correct prompts keeps growing during training (Figure 3b), so each batch gets weaker and noisier.
- Fix: over-sample, drop groups with accuracy 0 or 1, keep sampling until the batch is full.
  - Constraint in Equation 11: 0 < number of correct answers < G
- Costs more sampling, but reaches the same score in fewer steps (Figure 6).

## Fix 3: Token-level loss (Section 3.3)
| Sample-level (GRPO) | Token-level (DAPO) |
|---|---|
| Average the loss inside each answer, then across answers | Sum the loss over all tokens in the group, divide by the total token count |
| Every answer weighs the same | Every token weighs the same |
| Tokens in long answers count less | Long answers count in proportion to length |

- Long answers often hold gibberish and repetition. Sample-level loss under-punishes them: entropy and length grow unhealthily (Figure 4).
- Also: a good long answer's reasoning is learned properly.

## Fix 4: Overlong Reward Shaping (Section 3.4)
- Answers past the max length get cut off and punished, even if the reasoning was sound. That is reward noise.
- Step 1, Overlong Filtering: mask the loss of truncated answers. Training gets more stable (Figure 5).
- Step 2, Soft Overlong Punishment (Equation 13), added to the correctness reward:
  - length ≤ L_max − L_cache: 0
  - between: (L_max − L_cache − length) / L_cache, sliding from 0 to −1
  - length > L_max: −1
- Paper setting: L_max = 16,384, L_cache = 4,096, so up to 20,480 generated tokens.

## The full DAPO objective (Equations 8–12)
- J = (1 / Σ|o_i|) · Σ over answers Σ over tokens of min( ratio · Â, clip(ratio, 1 − ε_low, 1 + ε_high) · Â )
  - 1 / Σ|o_i|: divide by all tokens in the group (token-level loss)
  - ε_low, ε_high: separate clip bounds (Clip-Higher)
  - Â = (R_i − mean(R)) / std(R): same group advantage as GRPO
- Subject to: 0 < number of correct answers < G (Dynamic Sampling).
- No KL term.

## Data and training setup (Sections 3.5, 4.1)
- DAPO-Math-17K: 17K prompts, each with an integer answer.
  - Answers like a + b√c are rewritten so the answer becomes a + b + c: easy to check by rule
- AdamW, constant learning rate 1e-6, 20 warm-up rollout steps.
- 512 prompts per rollout, 16 answers each. Mini-batch 512 → 16 gradient updates per rollout step.
- Evaluation: AIME 2024 repeated 32 times, report avg@32. Temperature 1.0, top-p 0.7.

## DAPO ablation (Table 1, AIME 2024 avg@32, Qwen2.5-32B)
| Recipe | Score |
|---|---|
| DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B | 47 |
| Naive GRPO | 30 |
| + Overlong Filtering | 36 |
| + Clip-Higher | 38 |
| + Soft Overlong Punishment | 41 |
| + Token-level Loss | 42 |
| + Dynamic Sampling (DAPO) | 50 |

- Token-level loss adds the least score but makes training more stable and length growth healthier.

## What to watch during RL (Section 4.3, Figure 7)
- Response length: more room to explore; can stall or drop for a while. Read it with validation accuracy.
- Training reward: rises steadily, but has little link to validation accuracy. That means overfitting to the training set.
- Entropy: too low = no exploration; too high = gibberish. A slow upward trend helped.
- Small changes in data or settings can grow into large differences. Monitor everything.

## New reasoning appears (Section 4.4, Table 2)
- Early in training: almost no checking or reflecting on earlier steps.
- Later: the model writes things like "However, wait a moment, let's rethink about the dihedral angle..."
- RL did not only reinforce old patterns; it produced reflection and backtracking that were absent at the start.
- Compare DeepSeekMath's Maj@K finding: there, RL mostly sharpened what was already there.

# Running it on a free GPU

## A real run on a free GPU
- Model: SmolLM2-360M-Instruct. Train only a LoRA adapter.
  - LoRA: small add-on matrices instead of all weights; fits a free GPU
- Task: seeded arithmetic problems; answer read from the `Answer:` line.
- Rewards: +1.0 exact answer, +0.2 for the `Answer:` line, DAPO's soft overlong penalty × 0.5.
- Group size 8, 120 steps of 2 prompts, completions of at most 96 tokens.
- T4 has no bfloat16: set `bf16=False`, use fp16.

## Our STaR step
- 16 hard problems (e.g. "What is a * b - c + d?") sent to llama3.2:3b.
- Right on the first try → kept as "self".
- Wrong → retried with "(Hint: the correct answer is …)". If the new answer is right and never mentions the hint → kept as "rationalized", stored without the hint.
- Output: `star_sft.jsonl`. Fine-tuning on it is the last STaR step (Solution 3, with `SFTTrainer`).

## DAPO pieces in code
```python
def dynamic_sampling(groups):
    """Keep only groups whose rewards are not all equal (DAPO)."""
    return [g for g in groups if np.ptp(g) > 0]
def overlong_penalty(n_tokens, l_max=96, l_cache=32):
    if n_tokens <= l_max - l_cache:
        return 0.0
    if n_tokens <= l_max:
        return (l_max - l_cache - n_tokens) / l_cache
```
- Lines 1–3: `np.ptp` is max minus min. A group with spread 0 is dropped.
- Line 4: our sizes are tiny: 96-token limit, last 32 tokens are the penalty zone.
- Lines 5–6: up to 64 tokens, no penalty.
- Lines 7–8: from 64 to 96 tokens, the penalty slides from 0 to −1 (the notebook then returns −1.0 past 96).

# How this applies to us

## Contract checks are verifiable rewards
- Many contract checks have a code checker: right clause found, cap within 12 months, verdict line present.
- msa_20, 7.1 says 36 months. A checker compares the model's "Answer: no" to the label.
- That is a rule-based reward, as in DAPO. No reward model to get hacked.

## Train on the firm's own GPU
- GRPO needs no critic model; LoRA trains only small add-on weights.
- A small open model can be trained this way on one machine; no contract text leaves the firm.
- Use DAPO settings by default: no KL, clip-higher, token-level loss, length penalty.

## Pick the right questions
- Keep training questions at the edge of what the model can solve, so groups aren't all-equal.
- Yes/no questions are risky: STaR's authors warn that high-chance tasks give many right answers with bad reasoning.
  - Ask for the clause number and the quoted text too, and check both by code
- Hold out contracts the model never trained on, and compare accuracy before and after.

## Start with STaR, then RL
- STaR is just filtering plus normal fine-tuning: the cheapest first step.
- Rationalization: give the reviewer's correct flag as a hint, store the explanation without it.
- Then GRPO/DAPO: it also pushes wrong answers down, which STaR never does.
- Expect RL to make good answers more reliable (Maj@K), not to teach brand-new skills.

# Common mistakes

## Common mistakes
- Questions too easy or too hard → every group all-equal, zero advantages, nothing learned. Filter them out (dynamic sampling).
- A reward that can be gamed → check the answer format strictly, or reward it separately.
- Right answer, wrong reasoning → spot-check kept rationales, especially rationalized ones; avoid yes/no-only rewards.
- Raising temperature to get more training data → STaR found it worse than rationalization.
- bf16 on a T4 → set `bf16=False`; use fp16 or fp32.
- Reading training reward as the result → DAPO found it barely tracks validation accuracy. Compare held-out accuracy before and after.

## Check your understanding
**Q: A group of 4 answers gets rewards [1, 1, 1, 1]. What are the GRPO advantages, and what does DAPO do?**
A: All zero, so the group gives no gradient. DAPO's dynamic sampling drops it and samples another prompt.

**Q: In STaR, why is the hint removed before fine-tuning on a rationalized example?**
A: So the model learns to produce the reasoning from the question alone, as it must at test time.

**Q: What does GRPO use instead of PPO's critic model?**
A: The mean and spread of the rewards of the other answers sampled for the same prompt.

**Q: Why does DAPO raise only the upper clip bound to 0.28?**
A: With ε = 0.2, a token at 0.01 can only reach 0.012, so rare tokens can't grow and entropy collapses. Raising the lower bound would push rare tokens to 0 instead.

**Q: DeepSeekMath found RL raised Maj@K but not Pass@K. What does that mean?**
A: RL made right answers the model could already produce come out more often. It did not make new problems solvable.

**Q: Why does DAPO use a token-level loss instead of averaging per answer?**
A: Per-answer averaging makes tokens in long answers count less, so long gibberish isn't punished enough and length and entropy grow unhealthily.

**Q: Why does STaR fine-tune the original model each loop instead of the last one?**
A: To avoid overfitting. Each loop starts from the base model and trains on the newly collected data.
