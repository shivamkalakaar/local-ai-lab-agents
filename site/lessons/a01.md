## The big idea

You can make a model better without training it. Just let it try the same problem many times and keep a good answer. Extra work done at answer time is called test-time compute.

Think of a student on a hard exam question. One rushed attempt may be wrong. Five attempts on scrap paper give a much better chance that one of them is right. The catch: the student must then know which attempt to hand in.

That catch is the whole story of this module. More tries help only if something can tell the right try from the wrong ones. And the best way to spend those tries depends on how hard the question is.

## Why it matters for us

- On the firm's own computers, a small open model is cheap to run many times. Many cheap tries plus a good checker can beat one call to an expensive cloud model.
- Contract review often has checkable parts. Did the answer cite a real clause? Is the cap at most 12 months of fees? Code can check those. That makes extra tries worth it.
- Where nothing can check the answer, more tries mostly add cost. Build the checker first.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Repeated sampling.** Ask the same question many times at a temperature above 0. Temperature is a setting for how random the model's word choices are. At 0, every try is identical, so more tries add nothing.

2. **Coverage.** Coverage, also written pass@k, is the share of problems where at least one of k tries is right. The Large Language Monkeys paper (2024) sampled up to 10,000 times per problem. Coverage kept rising across four orders of magnitude. On a coding benchmark, one model went from 15.9% with one try to 56% with 250 tries.

3. **Picking is the hard part.** Coverage assumes a perfect checker that always finds the right try. Without one, the simplest picker is majority vote: keep the most common answer. In the same paper, on a math set, coverage passed 95%, but majority vote stalled near 40–41% after about 100 samples. The difference is called the generation-verification gap: the model could produce the right answer, but the picker can't find it.

4. **Why the curve has that shape.** The Power Laws paper (2025) explains it. For one problem, the chance that every try fails drops fast as you add tries. But averaged over many problems, the curve drops slowly. That's because a few problems are very hard, with almost no chance per try. This group of near-hopeless problems is called a heavy tail. The practical lesson: sample a little first, look at how often each problem succeeds, and forecast before spending.

5. **Build a pipeline of steps.** The Archon paper (2024) treats tricks as building blocks. A generator makes candidates. A ranker orders them and keeps the best. A fuser merges several into one better answer. A verifier keeps only answers that check out. Archon then searches for the best stack per task, instead of guessing.

6. **Spend by difficulty.** The Compute-Optimal paper (Snell et al., 2024) compared two ways to spend a budget. Parallel means many independent tries, then pick. Sequential means one try, then revise it, then revise again. Sequential worked best on easy questions. A mix worked best on harder ones. Choosing per question matched or beat simple best-of-N with up to 4x less compute. But on the hardest questions, a bigger model was the better buy.

| Strategy | What it does | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| Parallel | Many separate tries, then pick | Harder questions, with a good picker |
| Sequential | One try, then revise it | Easy questions |
| Bigger model | Pay for size, not tries | The hardest questions, or heavy traffic |

## Worked example

A paralegal asks the local model: "In msa_07, what is the liability cap in dollars?" The cap is a number of months times the monthly fee.

We ask 6 times at temperature 0.8. Say four tries give one figure and two give another. Majority vote picks the four. That is a fine guess, but not proof.

Now add a checker. Code can pull the clause numbers the answer cites and confirm the math. If only one try cites the right clause and multiplies correctly, the checker picks it even though it lost the vote. This is the generation-verification gap closing.

The notebook does the same with math word problems, which have exact answers. Here is how it scores majority vote:

```python
def majority_acc(probs, k):
    return sum(majority([s["answer"] for s in SAMPLES[p["id"]][:k]]) == p["answer"] for p in probs) / len(probs)
```

In plain words: for each problem, take the first k answers, keep the most common one, and check it against the true answer. Then report the share of problems it got right. The notebook prints this next to coverage, so you see the gap for every k.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting coverage as accuracy.** Coverage assumes you can always spot the right try. In real work you usually can't.
- **Adding samples without a checker.** Majority vote and reward models stalled near 100 samples while coverage kept rising.
- **Sampling at temperature 0.** Every try is the same. Turn the temperature up.
- **Counting calls instead of tokens.** A fuser or revision call reads all the candidates, so its cost grows with their number.
- **Picking a strategy on the same small set you report on.** That overfits. Hold some questions back for the final score.

## Check your understanding

**Q: Coverage at 100 tries is 90%, but majority vote at 100 tries is 45%. What is missing?**
A: A good verifier. The model can produce the right answer, but the picker can't find it. That gap is the generation-verification gap.

**Q: Why does the average success curve flatten even though each single problem gets solved quickly with more tries?**
A: A few problems are very hard, with almost no chance per try. These pull the average down and make it improve slowly.

**Q: For an easy question, should you spend 4 calls on 4 separate tries or on 1 try plus 3 revisions?**
A: Snell et al. found revision worked best on easy questions. They used a specially trained revision model, so test it on your own task.
