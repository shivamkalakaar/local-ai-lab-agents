## The big idea

Retrieval means finding the right few passages before the model answers. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) is the name for "search first, then answer from what you found."

Think of a law library. You don't hand a new associate every book and say "find the cap." You give them a good index. They pull the three pages that matter, read those, and answer. The model is the associate. Retrieval is the index.

The default that works has a few parts. Split documents on their natural structure. Label each piece with where it came from. Search two ways: by exact words and by meaning. Merge the results. Then check every quote the model gives against the real text.

## Why it matters for us

A firm has many contracts. They won't all fit on the model's desk at once, and small local models do worse with long prompts. Retrieval lets the model read only the clauses that matter.

If all your documents are small, you may not need retrieval. The course's rule of thumb: under about 200K tokens of documents, put it all in the prompt and skip it. Above that, build the pipeline.

Lawyers need answers they can trust. A citation that points to a real clause, checked by code, is what makes an answer usable. Everything here, including the search model, runs on the firm's own machine.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Chunk on structure.** A chunk is one piece of a document. For contracts, one section makes a natural chunk. Splitting on headings keeps each idea whole.

2. **Add a context note.** Put a one-line label at the top of each chunk, like "From msa_07, section 7 (Limitation of Liability)." The clause text alone never says which contract it came from. The label does. This idea is called contextual retrieval (from Anthropic).

3. **Search by keywords.** BM25 is a classic keyword search. It scores chunks by shared words. It is good at exact strings like "7.2(b)" or "msa_15".

4. **Search by meaning.** An embedding model turns text into a list of numbers that captures meaning. Similar meanings give similar numbers. This finds "limitation of liability" when the lawyer typed "cap on damages."

5. **Merge with RRF.** Reciprocal rank fusion combines two ranked lists. Each chunk gets points for ranking high in either list: 1 divided by (60 plus its rank). Add the points and sort.

6. **Optionally rerank.** Fetch a wide pool, then let a slower, more accurate model reorder it and keep the best few.

7. **Check citations in code.** The model must name its source chunk and give an exact quote. Code confirms the quote is really in that chunk.

```
question --> keyword search  --\
                                 merge (RRF) --> top chunks --> model answers --> code checks quote
question --> meaning search  --/
```

- The second line joins them into one block, each tagged with its ID like `[msa_20#7]`.
- The third line asks the model for an answer, a source ID and an exact quote, in a fixed shape.
- The fourth line finds the chunk the model named among the ones we gave it.
- The last line checks that the chunk exists and the quote really appears in it.

## Common mistakes

- **Using meaning search alone.** It misses exact strings like clause numbers and IDs. Use keyword search too.
- **Judging retrieval only by the final answer.** If the answer is wrong, you can't tell which part failed. Measure recall@k separately.
- **Treating retrieved text as instructions.** It is data. A contract could contain "ignore previous instructions."
- **Ignoring who may see what.** Filter by client or matter during the search, not after.

## Check your understanding

**Q: A lawyer asks "what does 7.2(b) say?" and meaning search returns the wrong clause. Why?**
A: Embeddings capture meaning, not exact strings like "7.2(b)". Keyword search finds it. That is why we search both ways.

**Q: Why add a context note to each chunk?**
A: The clause text alone doesn't say which contract or section it belongs to. The note carries that, which helps both kinds of search.

**Q: Why fetch a wide pool and then rerank, instead of fetching only a few?**
A: Fast search is noisy. A wide pool makes sure the right passage is in there. The slower reranker then moves it to the top.
