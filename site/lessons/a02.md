## The big idea

In a01, more tries meant a better chance that one try was right. But someone still has to find that try. A verifier is anything that scores a candidate answer. It can be exact code, unit tests, a trained model, an AI judge, or a simple rule.

Think of a firm reviewing a junior's memo. A partner can check only the conclusion. Or she can read each step and mark where the reasoning slipped. She can also ask three colleagues, each good at spotting different errors, and weigh their views.

Those are the three ideas in this module. Check the outcome. Check each step. And combine several weak checkers into one strong one.

## Why it matters for us

- A small local model can often produce a right answer in a few tries. The verifier decides whether you actually ship it. Without one, extra tries are wasted money.
- Contract review has many cheap checks. Does a cited clause exist? Is there a clear "sign" or "don't sign" verdict? Do the numbers add up? Each is weak alone, but together they are strong.
- Step scores also help the lawyer. They point to the exact line where an answer went wrong.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Score a verifier like a filter.** A verifier accepts or rejects each candidate. Precision asks: of what it accepted, how much was really right? Recall asks: of the right answers, how many did it accept? When you must pick one answer, precision matters most. A judge that says yes to everything is useless for picking.

2. **Outcome verifiers.** An outcome verifier judges only the final answer. The GSM8K paper (Cobbe et al., 2021) trained one, then picked the best of 100 samples. It gave about the same boost as a 30x larger model. But there was a warning. With their 6B verifier, accuracy fell past 400 samples. Search started finding wrong answers that fooled the verifier. This is called reward hacking.

3. **Process verifiers.** A process verifier scores each step. The "Let's Verify Step by Step" paper (Lightman et al., 2023) had people label 800,000 steps. A solution's score is the chance that every step is right: multiply the step scores. Picking the best of 1,860 solutions on a math set, step checking got 78.2%. Outcome checking got 72.4%. Majority vote got 69.6%.

4. **Step labels without people.** Labeling steps by hand is expensive. The Math-Shepherd paper (2023) found a shortcut. Cut a solution after step i. Let a model finish it several times (8 in their data). Check the final answers. The share that come out right is the soft label. "Did any come out right?" is the hard label. If a step is wrong, the finishes after it start failing. Training on these labels lifted a 7B model from 77.9% to 84.1% on a grade-school math set.

5. **Combine weak verifiers.** The Weaver paper (2025) started from many imperfect checkers. It turned each score into a yes/no vote. Then it estimated how accurate each checker is without true answers, from how much it agrees with the others. This is called weak supervision. Checkers that look no better than chance get dropped. The rest are weighted by accuracy. Weaver averaged 87.7% across four test sets, against 72.2% for majority vote. It then shrank the whole ensemble into a small model that kept about 98% of the accuracy at a tiny fraction of the cost.

| Verifier | Looks at | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome | Final answer | Simple | Can be fooled at scale |
| Process | Each step | Shows where it broke | Costly labels |
| Weak ensemble | Many checks | No labels needed | Twins with the same blind spot |

## Worked example

The firm asks the local model four times: "Can we sign msa_15 as written?" We get four answers. Now we score each with three cheap checkers.

- **Agreement:** what share of the other answers reached the same verdict.
- **AI judge:** the model rates the answer from 0 to 10.
- **Code check:** do the cited clause numbers exist, and is there a clear verdict?

Say the judge gives every answer 9. It can't help us pick. The agreement and code checks disagree on one answer. The weighting gives the judge little say, because it looks no better than chance. The answer with real citations and support from the others wins.

The notebook does the same with math problems. Here are its three checkers as yes/no votes:

```python
VERIFIERS = {"judge": lambda c: c["judge"] >= 0.7,
             "agree": lambda c: c["agree"] >= 0.5,
             "arith": lambda c: c["arith"] >= 0.75}
```

In plain words: the judge votes yes if it scored 7 or more out of 10. Agreement votes yes if at least half the other tries reached the same answer. The arithmetic check recomputes every "a op b = c" in the text with Python, and votes yes if at least 75% are right. The notebook then weights these votes without using the true answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting a judge that says yes to almost everything.** High recall with low precision can't pick one answer. Measure precision first.
- **Searching too hard against one verifier.** Past some number of samples, wrong answers start to fool it. Cap the samples.
- **Combining checkers with the same blind spot.** Two prompts to the same model will agree with each other. Label-free weighting then overrates both. Mix checkers that fail in different ways.
- **Using too few rollouts for step labels.** Each finish moves a soft label by a big jump when there are few. Use more, or treat the labels as rough.
- **Fitting weights on your test set.** Use a separate set, or a label-free method.

## Check your understanding

**Q: Why can majority vote fail even when the right answer was sampled?**
A: If the right answer is rare and wrong answers cluster on the same value, the vote picks the cluster.

**Q: What does a process verifier give you that an outcome verifier doesn't?**
A: A score for each step, so you see where a solution broke. Lightman et al. found it also picked better: 78.2% against 72.4%.

**Q: How does Math-Shepherd label a step without people?**
A: It finishes the solution from that step several times. The share of right final answers is the soft label; whether any is right is the hard label.
