## Learning objective
- Measure an agent in human terms: task length it can finish, expert preference for its work, and separate scores for coverage and citations.

## Three questions, three papers
| Question | Paper | Number |
|---|---|---|
| How long a task can it finish? | METR (Kwa et al. 2025) | 50% time horizon, in human minutes |
| Would an expert prefer its work? | GDPval (Patwardhan et al. 2025) | Win-or-tie rate vs an expert |
| Is its research report sound? | DeepScholar-Bench (Patel et al. 2025) | Separate coverage, retrieval, citation scores |

- "78% on a benchmark" doesn't tell a customer what the agent can do.

## METR: time horizon
- Time skilled humans on 170 tasks: over 800 baselines, 2,529 hours.
- Human baseline: a timed attempt; task difficulty = geometric mean of successful times.
- Per model, fit a logistic curve (S-shaped, 0 to 1): success vs log of human time.
- 50% time horizon: task length where the curve crosses 50%.
- 8 runs per agent and task.

## 50% vs 80% horizon
| 50% horizon | 80% horizon |
|---|---|
| Tasks the model finishes half the time | Tasks it finishes 80% of the time |
| Claude 3.7 Sonnet: about 50 minutes | Roughly 4–6x shorter than the 50% horizon |
| Doubled about every 7 months since 2019 | Closer to what business use needs |

- Half of tasks failing is not a product. Quote the 80% (or higher) number.

## Our toy: multi-step contract checks
- A task with N steps: walk through N facts from a contract fact sheet, keep a running total.
  - "Add the notice days of msa_..." / "Subtract the renewal years of msa_..."
- 15 tasks, 1 to 32 steps. Each run twice (METR: 8 times).
- Assumed human time: 1 minute + 1.5 minutes per step (2.5 to 49 minutes).
  - Our guess. METR timed real people. The horizon is only as good as these times.

## GDPval: grade the real deliverable
- 1,320 tasks, 44 occupations, 9 sectors contributing most to US GDP.
- Written by professionals with 14 years of experience on average.
- Deliverable: the actual work product (document, slides, spreadsheet).
- Experts compare model vs expert deliverables, blind and pairwise.
- Win-or-tie rate: share judged as good as or better than the expert's.

## Count review time
| Raw comparison (GPT-5) | "Try once, then fix" |
|---|---|
| About 90x faster than an expert | About 1.12x faster |
| About 474x cheaper | About 1.18x cheaper |
| Ignores review | Expert reviews and fixes or redoes the output |

- Automated grader agreed with experts 65.7% of the time; experts with each other 70.8%.

## Order swap in code
```python
first = grade_once(cid, model_text, expert_text)     # model is A
second = grade_once(cid, expert_text, model_text)    # model is B
if first == "A" and second == "B": return "win"
if first == "B" and second == "A": return "loss"
return "tie"
```
- Lines 1–2: grade the same pair twice, positions swapped.
- Line 3: model picked both times → win.
- Line 4: expert picked both times → loss.
- Line 5: any disagreement → tie. Small graders often pick the same position both times.

## Expert email vs wrong email (msa_17)
| Expert reference | Confidently wrong |
|---|---|
| "Clause 4.1 ... renews automatically for five-year periods; the playbook allows one year at most." | "It meets every playbook rule, so the client can sign it as is." |
| "It also needs only 15 days' notice of non-renewal; the playbook requires at least 30 days." | |
| "Recommendation: revise 4.1 to one-year renewals with at least 30 days' notice." | |

- Contract 4.1: "...successive five (5) year periods unless either party gives at least 15 days' prior written notice..."
- Sanity check: the wrong email must not win against the expert.

## DeepScholar-Bench: three axes
- Live benchmark: queries come from recent arXiv papers, so models can't have trained on them.
- Task: research and write a cited related-work section.
- Knowledge synthesis: organization; nugget coverage (key facts from the human-written section).
- Retrieval quality: relevance; reference coverage (important citations found).
- Verifiability: citation precision; claim coverage.

## Each metric catches a different failure
| Metric | What it checks | Drops when... |
|---|---|---|
| Reference coverage | Share of relevant sources cited | a key source is never cited |
| Retrieval precision | Share of cited sources that are relevant | a distractor (MemGPT, CacheBlend) is cited |
| Citation precision | Cited source supports its sentence | a real source is cited for a claim it doesn't make |
| Claim coverage | Every sentence backed by a citation | a sentence has no citation |

- Our "supports" test: at least 3 shared content words. The paper uses an LLM judge.

## What the papers showed
| Paper | What it showed |
|---|---|
| METR | 50% horizons doubled about every 7 months (207 days); GPT-2 about 2 seconds, Claude 3.7 Sonnet about 50 minutes |
| GDPval | Best on the gold subset: Claude Opus 4.1, 47.6% win-or-tie vs experts |
| DeepScholar-Bench | No system above a 31% geometric mean; OpenAI DeepResearch 0.309 but claim coverage 0.138 |

## How this applies to us
- A firm needs a plain claim: "finishes X-minute contract checks Y% of the time". Time real lawyers on real tasks.
- Every agent output at a firm is reviewed. Count review time in any savings claim.
- Contract summaries cite clauses. Check each citation supports its sentence.

## Common mistakes
- Guessing human times → time real people; the horizon depends on them.
- Quoting the 50% horizon → quote 80% or more, and plan review around it.
- One-order LLM grader → swap positions; count disagreement as a tie.
- Rewarding copied text → word-overlap metrics can be gamed by pasting sources; keep a judge for synthesis.

## Check your understanding
**Q: How does METR turn success rates into a time horizon?**
A: For each model, fit a logistic curve of success against log human time, then find the task length where it crosses 50%.

**Q: Why did GDPval's speed and cost advantage shrink?**
A: An expert must review the model's output and fix or redo it when it is wrong. That review time counts.

**Q: A summary cites a distractor source for a true sentence. Which of our metrics drops?**
A: Retrieval precision (a non-relevant source was cited), and probably citation precision (the source doesn't support the sentence).
