## The big idea

"78% on a benchmark" tells a customer almost nothing. Which 78%? Of what? Would the work be usable? This lesson measures agents in human terms instead.

Think of hiring a paralegal. You don't ask for their test score. You ask: how big a task can I hand them and expect it done? Would a senior lawyer accept their memo? When they cite a source, does the source really say that?

Three papers turn those questions into numbers. METR asks how long a task (in human minutes) the agent finishes half the time. GDPval asks whether an expert would prefer the agent's work over another expert's. DeepScholar-Bench scores a research report on several separate axes, so you can see exactly what went wrong.

## Why it matters for us

- A firm deciding whether to trust a local agent needs a plain claim, like "it finishes 10-minute contract checks 80% of the time". A benchmark percentage won't do.
- Every agent output at a law firm gets reviewed. Review time must be counted in any claim about savings.
- Contract summaries cite clauses. You need to check that each citation actually supports the sentence it sits next to.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Time real people first** (the METR paper, 2025). METR timed skilled people on 170 tasks, from seconds to many hours. A task's difficulty is how long people took to do it. Guessing these times would ruin everything that follows.

2. **Fit an S-curve.** For each model, plot success against task length. Longer tasks fail more. A logistic curve (an S-shaped curve running from 0 to 1) fits this well. The 50% time horizon is the task length where the curve crosses 50%. METR found frontier models' 50% horizons doubled about every 7 months since 2019. Claude 3.7 Sonnet was about 50 minutes.

3. **Quote the reliable number.** Half of tasks failing is not a product. The 80% horizon is the length a model finishes 80% of the time. METR found it was roughly 4 to 6 times shorter than the 50% horizon. That is the number a business should plan around.

4. **Grade the real work product** (the GDPval paper, 2025). GDPval has 1,320 tasks across 44 occupations, written by professionals with about 14 years of experience on average. Each asks for a real deliverable (a memo, a spreadsheet, slides). Experts compare the model's version with a human expert's, blind and side by side. The score is the win-or-tie rate: how often the model's work was judged as good or better. The best model on their gold set, Claude Opus 4.1, reached 47.6%.

5. **Count review time.** GDPval found GPT-5 was about 90 times faster than an expert on raw time. But someone must review the output and fix it when it's wrong. Counting that, the gain shrank to about 1.12 times faster and 1.18 times cheaper. An automatic grader agreed with experts 65.7% of the time, against 70.8% between experts. Useful for quick checks, not a replacement.

6. **Score research reports on separate axes** (the DeepScholar-Bench paper, 2025). Systems write a cited related-work section for a recent paper. It is a live benchmark (new items keep being added, so models can't have trained on them). Scores cover three axes: synthesis (did it include the key facts?), retrieval (did it find the important sources?) and verifiability (does each citation support its sentence?). No system passed 31% on the combined score. The best system overall was weak on citations, while another led on citations.

## Worked example

The notebook builds all three on contracts.

**Time horizon.** A task with N steps asks the agent to walk through N facts from a contract fact sheet, keeping a running total. More steps means more chances to slip. The notebook assumes a person needs 1 minute to set up plus 1.5 minutes per step. That is a made-up estimate, and it says so. Each task runs twice. Then it fits the S-curve and reads off the 50% and 80% horizons.

**Expert comparison.** Three client emails about contracts that break the playbook. For example, a liability cap above 12 months of fees. An expert reference email is written from the labels. A model grader compares the agent's email with the expert's, in both orders. Small graders often pick whichever email comes first. So if the verdict flips when the order swaps, it counts as a tie.

**Research report.** The agent gets six sources: three about evaluating agents, three about memory (distractors). It writes a short cited summary. Plain code then checks each metric:

- Citing a distractor lowers retrieval precision.
- A sentence with no citation lowers claim coverage (the share of sentences backed by a source).
- Citing a real source for a claim it doesn't make lowers citation precision.

One overall score would hide which of these went wrong.

## Common mistakes

- **Guessing human times.** The horizon is only as good as the timings. Time real people where you can.
- **Quoting the 50% horizon to a customer.** Quote 80% or higher, and design review around it.
- **Claiming big speed-ups without review time.** GDPval's gain nearly vanished once review and fixes were counted.
- **Trusting a grader that saw only one order.** Swap the order and count a flip as a tie.
- **Rewarding copied text.** Word-overlap scores can be gamed by pasting sources. Keep a judge for whether it is a real synthesis.

## Check your understanding

**Q: How does METR turn success rates into a time horizon?**
A: For each model it fits an S-curve of success against the log of human task time. The horizon is the task length where the curve crosses 50%.

**Q: Why did GDPval's speed and cost advantage shrink so much?**
A: An expert must review the model's output and fix or redo it when it is wrong. Once that time is counted, most of the raw speed gain disappears.

**Q: A summary cites a memory paper for a sentence about agent evaluation. Which metrics drop?**
A: Retrieval precision, because a non-relevant source was cited. Probably citation precision too, because that source doesn't support the sentence.
