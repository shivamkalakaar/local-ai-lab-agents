Robust verification

Open in Colab

Verifiers score answers so you can pick the right try. Check whole answers or each step, and combine weak checkers.

Learning objective

1/58
  • Learn how to build and score verifiers, why step checks beat answer checks, how to make step labels without people, and how to combine weak checkers into a strong one.
Part 1Why this lecture exists

Why verification?

2/58
  • a01: coverage grew fast with more tries, but majority vote kept only part of the gain.
  • Verifier: anything that scores a candidate answer.
    • Exact checker, unit tests, a trained model, an LLM judge, a rule
  • Generation-verification gap: coverage minus what your verifier actually picks.
  • No verifier → more tries mostly add cost.

Scoring a verifier

3/58
  • A verifier accepts or rejects each candidate. Score it like a classifier.
  • Precision: of the accepted candidates, how many were right.
  • Recall: of the right candidates, how many it accepted.
  • Oracle: compares with the true answer. Perfect, but rare in business work.
  • To pick one answer, precision matters more than recall.

Outcome vs process verifiers

4/58
Outcome reward model (ORM) Process reward model (PRM)
Judges only the final answer Scores each reasoning step
One score per solution Solution score = product (or minimum) of step scores
Says "wrong" Says where it went wrong

Four papers, one story

5/58
Paper What it adds
Training Verifiers (Cobbe et al. 2021) GSM8K; a trained outcome verifier + best-of-100
Let's Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al. 2023) Human step labels (PRM800K); PRM beats ORM
Math-Shepherd (Wang et al. 2023) Step labels from rollouts, no people; PRM for reranking and RL
Weaver (Saad-Falcon et al. 2025) Combine many weak verifiers without labels; distill to a small model
Part 2Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems (Cobbe et al. 2021)

The problem

6/58
  • Large models still fail at multi-step math. One mistake ruins the solution.
  • Autoregressive models (write one token at a time) can't go back and fix errors.
  • Scaling alone looks too expensive:
    • Log-linear extrapolation: about 10^16 parameters to reach 80% on GSM8K with fine-tuning.
    • 175B model would need at least two more orders of magnitude of data.
  • Idea: train a verifier to judge solutions, then pick the best of many samples.
  • Verification is often easier than generation.

The GSM8K dataset

7/58
  • 8.5K grade-school math word problems written by people: 7.5K train, 1K test.
  • 2 to 8 steps each; only + − × ÷.
  • Design principles:
    • High quality: under 2 percent of problems have breaking errors (estimated).
    • High diversity: no shared templates.
    • Moderate difficulty: hard for big models, not hopeless.
    • Natural-language solutions, not just equations.

Two methods compared (Section 4)

8/58
Fine-tuning (baseline) Verification
Train the model on solutions Also train a separate verifier
Test: one sample at T = 0 Test: 100 samples at higher temperature
Check that one answer Verifier scores each; return the top one
  • Models: GPT-3 family, mostly 6B and 175B.
  • Calculator: training data has calculation annotations; at test time a calculator overrides the model's arithmetic.

Fine-tuning and coverage (Figure 3)

9/58
  • test@N: share of problems solved at least once in N guesses (T = 0 for test@1, T = 0.7 for test@100).
  • test@1 keeps improving with more epochs.
  • test@100 peaks in the first few epochs, then drops sharply.
    • The model gets overconfident, so its samples stop covering different solutions.
  • So the generator for verifier data is trained only 2 epochs.
  • Writing out steps matters: answer-only 6B model drops from 20.6% to 5.2%.

How the verifier is trained (Figure 4)

10/58
    1. Fine-tune a generator for 2 epochs.
    1. Sample 100 solutions per training problem; label each right or wrong by its final answer.
    1. Train a verifier for 1 epoch on these.
  • Verifier output: probability the solution is correct.
  • Extra objective: the verifier also does language modeling (predict the next token).
  • Weakness: right answer with wrong reasoning gets labeled "correct" (false positives).

Results (Figure 5)

11/58
  • Small training sets: verification does not help. It overfits to the right answer.
  • Enough data: a strong boost over fine-tuning.
  • Full dataset: 6B verification slightly beats a fine-tuned 175B model.
    • About the same as a 30x larger model.
  • 175B verifiers "take off" earlier: need fewer problems to pass the baseline.
  • Verification scales better with more data than fine-tuning does.

Verifier ablations (Figure 6)

12/58
Choice Finding
Token-level vs solution-level Token-level (a score after every token) trains slower but wins and overfits less
Verification only vs + language modeling Adding language modeling is a strict improvement
Bigger generator vs bigger verifier Large generator + small verifier beats small generator + large verifier
  • Token-level verifier: like a value function, judging the reasoning all the way through.
  • A small verifier works well, so it may use coarse heuristics, not full checking.

More samples is not always better (Figure 7)

13/58
  • 6B verifier: accuracy rises up to 400 completions per problem, then falls.
    • Search starts finding wrong answers that fool the verifier.
  • Default: 100 completions, most of the gain at modest cost.
  • Vote among the top-ranked samples instead of taking only the top one:
    • 100 samples: let the top 3–5 vote.
    • 3200 samples: let about the top 30 vote.

Regularization (Section 5.2)

14/58
  • Residual dropout: randomly zero 20% of each layer's residual path during training.
  • Helps fine-tuning and verification a lot.
  • Solution-level verifiers with dropout reach about the level of token-level verifiers.
  • Token-level verifiers gain only slightly; they already overfit less.
  • GPT-3 was not pretrained with dropout, so they pretrained a bit more with it first.
Part 3Let's Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al. 2023)

The problem

15/58
  • Even the best models make logical mistakes; one error derails a long solution.
  • Outcome supervision: feedback on the final result only.
  • Process supervision: feedback on every step.
  • Earlier work (Uesato et al. 2022) found both similar on grade-school math.
  • This paper: a stronger base model (GPT-4), far more human feedback, harder data (MATH).

Why process supervision appeals

16/58
  • More precise: it says exactly where the error is.
  • Easier for people to read and check.
  • Rewards a human-endorsed chain of thought, not just the answer.
  • ORM-trained models often reach right answers with wrong reasoning.

Setup (Section 2)

17/58
  • Large scale: all models fine-tuned from base GPT-4 (no RLHF).
  • Small scale: similar models with roughly 200 times less pretraining compute.
  • All models also trained on MathMix: about 1.5B math-related tokens.
  • Generator: taught to write one step per line, so steps are easy to split.
  • No RL on the generator. Reward models are judged by best-of-N search only.

Collecting step labels (Section 2.4)

18/58
  • People label each step of GPT-4's solutions:
    • Positive: correct and reasonable
    • Negative: incorrect or unreasonable
    • Neutral: ambiguous, e.g. subtly misleading
  • PRM800K: 800K step labels across 75K solutions to 12K problems.
  • 4.5K MATH test problems went into training, so tests use the other 500.

Show people the convincing wrong answers

19/58
  • Showing obviously wrong solutions wastes labels.
  • Instead surface "convincing wrong-answer" solutions:
    • Convincing: rated highly by the current best PRM
    • Wrong-answer: final answer is wrong
  • The PRM must be wrong about at least one step, so each label teaches something.
  • Retrain the PRM on the latest data several times during collection.

Training ORM and PRM (Sections 2.5–2.6)

20/58
ORM PRM
Uniform samples, labeled by final answer Human step labels from PRM800K
Score = prediction at the final token One prediction after each step
100 samples per problem; no overlap with PRM800K Supervised only up to the first wrong step
  • PRM solution score = probability every step is correct = product of step probabilities.
  • Labels stop at the first mistake, so ORM and PRM get about the same information on correct solutions.

A fair comparison at small scale (Section 4.1)

22/58
  • Problem: large-scale ORM and PRM had different training sets.
  • Fix: use the big PRM (PRM_large) as a cheap stand-in labeler for small models.
  • Same data, three kinds of labels:
    • Process labels from PRM_large
    • Outcome labels from PRM_large
    • Outcome labels from final-answer checking
  • Figure 4a (best-of-500): process supervision wins at every data size.
  • Figure 4b: PRM_large outcome labels beat final-answer labels; they catch right answers with wrong reasoning.

Active learning (Section 4.2)

23/58
  • PRM_selector: a small PRM trained on one sample per problem; scores 1000 samples per problem.
  • Choose N per problem: 80% most convincing wrong-answer, 20% most convincing of the rest.
  • Result: about 2.6x more data-efficient than uniform labeling (from line-of-best-fit slopes).
  • At 200 samples per problem it slightly underperforms the trend (200 is a big share of the 1000 pool).
  • Retraining the selector during collection was unstable; no gain.

Why it works, and what it costs (Section 6)

25/58
  • Credit assignment: an ORM must figure out where a wrong solution failed. Hard when most solutions have some error.
  • A PRM is told how many steps were fine and which step broke.
  • Negative alignment tax: the safer method (rewarding good reasoning) also performs better.
  • Limits: possible MATH test contamination (they argue it affects all methods alike); math only.
Part 4Math-Shepherd (Wang et al. 2023)

The problem

26/58
  • PRMs beat ORMs (Lightman et al.), but human step labels are expensive.
  • Labeling complex math steps needs skilled annotators.
  • Goal: build PRM training data automatically.
  • Use it two ways:
    • Verification: rerank many candidate answers
    • Reinforcement learning: step-by-step PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, a standard RL method)

ORM and PRM losses (Equations 1–2)

27/58
  • ORM loss = y_s · log r_s + (1 − y_s) · log(1 − r_s)
    • y_s: 1 if the solution's answer is right, else 0
    • r_s: the ORM's score (0 to 1)
    • Standard cross-entropy: push r_s toward y_s
  • PRM loss = the same, summed over steps i = 1..K, with step labels y_si and step scores r_si.
  • Binary step labels worked about as well as Lightman's good/neutral/bad.

Label a step by finishing it (Section 3.3)

28/58
  • Quality of a step: its potential to reach the correct answer.
  • Completer: a model that finishes the solution from step i, N times.
  • Check each finished answer against the golden answer.
  • Hard estimate (HE): 1 if any finish is right, else 0.
  • Soft estimate (SE): share of finishes that are right.
  • Inspired by Monte Carlo tree search.

A worked example (Figure 2)

29/58
Outcome label Process label for step 1
Whole solution answers 20; golden answer 24 Finish from step 1 three times: 24, 24, 20
y = 0 for the whole solution Soft = 2/3; hard = 1
  • The outcome label blames every step. The process label sees step 1 was on track.

Ranking and RL (Sections 3.4–3.5)

30/58
  • Solution score = minimum step score (following Lightman et al.).
  • Self-consistency + RM: group solutions by final answer, sum RM scores per group, pick the top group.
    • answer = argmax over a of Σ_i 1{a_i = a} · RM(p, S_i) (Equation 5)
  • Step-by-step PPO: reward at the end of each step, not just at the end of the answer.

Setup (Section 4)

31/58
  • Generators and completer trained on MetaMATH.
  • Data: 7B and 13B models sample 15 solutions per problem; duplicates removed.
  • Completer: LLemma-7B, N = 8 finishes per step.
  • About 170k labeled solutions for GSM8K and 270k for MATH.
  • PRM trained on hard labels (two special tokens: "has potential" / "no potential").
  • Verification: 256 candidates per problem; MATH500 = Lightman's 500-problem test set.

Model size and new data

37/58

38/58

  • Labels are noisy, like outcome labels.
  • Gap between PRM800K and open-model outputs makes a clean human-vs-automatic comparison hard.
  • Suggestion: combine human and automatic step labels.
Part 5Weaver: combining weak verifiers (Saad-Falcon et al. 2025)

The problem

39/58
  • Strong verifiers are rare: people don't scale; Lean only fits formal proofs.
  • LLM judges and reward models are general but weak: noisy, poorly calibrated, many false positives.
  • Three challenges:
    • Naive averaging lets bad verifiers drag the score down.
    • Learning weights needs labels, which are expensive.
    • Verification is costly: 10× to 128× the compute per query to beat majority vote.

Definitions (Section 3)

40/58
  • K: responses sampled per query.
  • Pass@K: share of queries where at least one response is right. Ignores the picker.
  • Success rate: share of queries where the picked response is right.
  • Generation-verification gap = Pass@K − success rate.
  • D_dev: a small labeled set, 1% of the data (e.g. 5 to 10 queries), used only for global statistics.

Weighted beats naive (Section 4.1)

41/58
  • Setup: Llama 3.3 70B Instruct generates; 33 reward models and LLM judges (7B–72B) verify.
  • Individual verifiers differ a lot: success rates span up to 37.5%.
  • Weighted ensembles with all labels: up to 11.2 points over naive averaging.
    • Figure 2: top-K verifiers +3.6%, supervised weights +7.8% on average.
  • But with only 1% labels, the supervised weights lose 20.1% accuracy on average.
  • So: how to weight without many labels?

Weaver step 1: normalize and binarize

42/58
  • Normalize: min-max scale each verifier to [0, 1]: s′ = (s − min) / (max − min).
  • Binarize: turn each score into a yes/no vote, with a threshold set from the small dev set.
    • Judges already say yes/no; reward models give continuous scores.
Binarization (Table 4) Average accuracy
Naive 0.5 threshold 79.0%
Threshold from 1% dev set 84.5%
Threshold from 5% dev set 87.5%

Weaver step 2: drop low-signal verifiers

43/58
  • Drop verifiers whose yes-rate is extreme for the task:
    • Class balance 20–80%: drop verifiers with yes-rates outside 20–80%.
    • Under 20% right answers: drop verifiers that say yes over 80% of the time.
    • Over 80% right answers: drop verifiers that say yes under 20% of the time.
  • Also helps the math: weak supervision assumes most verifiers beat chance.
Filtering (Table 5) Average accuracy
No verifiers dropped 79.4%
Extreme yes-rates dropped 85.2%

Weaver step 3: the weak supervision model (Equation 1)

44/58
  • Assume verifiers are independent given the true label Y (each catches different aspects).
  • Pr(Y = 1 | votes) = Π_i Pr(S_i = s_i | Y = 1) · Pr(Y = 1) / Pr(votes)
    • Pr(Y = 1): share of right answers, from the dev set
    • Pr(S_i = 1 | Y = 1): verifier i's accuracy, unknown
    • Pr(votes): how often this vote pattern appears
  • Pick the response with the highest posterior.

Estimating accuracies without labels (Equations 2–5)

45/58
  • Two verifiers' joint vote rates must equal a mix of their accuracies:
    • Pr(S_i, S_j) = Pr(S_i|Y=1)·Pr(S_j|Y=1)·Pr(Y=1) + Pr(S_i|Y=0)·Pr(S_j|Y=0)·Pr(Y=0)
  • Each verifier's yes-rate must too: Pr(S_i = 1) = Pr(S_i=1|Y=1)·Pr(Y=1) + Pr(S_i=1|Y=0)·Pr(Y=0).
  • Left sides are counted from the votes; right sides contain the unknown accuracies.
  • Minimize the mismatch with gradient descent → accuracy estimates → weights.
  • Optional: separate models per difficulty bucket (Appendix B.4).

Scaling the number of verifiers (Figure 4)

48/58
  • Add verifiers from most to least accurate, 1 to 15.
  • Up to 8.5% over the single best verifier.
  • Weaver beats naive averaging by +2.4% to +10.1% (GPQA Diamond +10.1%, MMLU Pro +5.1%).
  • Gains shrink as more are added: later verifiers share the same blind spots on hard examples.
  • More verifiers beat more scores from the same verifier (prompt or temperature variation).

Distill into a small model (Section 6)

49/58
  • Train a cross-encoder (reads query + response together, outputs one score) on Weaver's scores.
  • Model: ModernBERT-Large, 396M parameters.
  • Cost per query, 100 samples: full Weaver 35.35 exaFLOPs; cross-encoder 1.01 exaFLOPs.
  • Keeps 98.2% of Weaver's performance (abstract: 98.7%), saving 99.97% of verifier FLOPs.
  • Beats majority vote by 23.2% for 0.57% more compute than generation alone.
  • One A100 GPU instead of an 8-GPU node per 70B verifier.

Limitations (Section 7)

50/58
  • Very hard datasets (e.g. AIMO 2024): so few right answers that Weaver struggles to pick one.
    • More generations help by raising the count of right answers.
  • Independence assumption: verifiers that share blind spots look more accurate than they are.
  • Full ensemble needs 30+ verifier calls per response before distillation.
  • Future: specialized verifiers, Weaver labels for RLHF, multimodal verification.
Part 6The notebook in code

Three weak verifiers in code

51/58
VERIFIERS = {"judge": lambda c: c["judge"] >= 0.7, "agree": lambda c: c["agree"] >= 0.5, "arith": lambda c: c["arith"] >= 0.75}
VOTES = {c["key"]: {v: int(f(c)) for v, f in VERIFIERS.items()} for c in CANDS}
  • judge: yes if the LLM judge scored the answer 7 or more out of 10.
  • agree: yes if at least half of the other tries reached the same answer.
  • arith: yes if at least 75% of the "a op b = c" lines recompute correctly in Python.
  • VOTES: one yes/no vote per verifier per candidate; weighted next without labels.

Math-Shepherd labels in code

52/58

        hits = sum(extract_int(o) == p["answer"] for o in outs)

  • prefix: the solution cut after step i.
  • outs: the model finishes it ROLLOUTS = 3 times, in parallel.

Label-free weights in code

53/58
        a = min(0.95, max(0.05, acc_fn(v)))
        out[v] = math.log(a / (1 - a)) if a >= min_acc else 0.0     # filter: weak-looking verifiers get no say
  • acc_fn(v): estimated accuracy; without labels, how well v agrees with the other verifiers.
  • Clamp to 5–95% so no verifier gets infinite weight.
  • Weight = log(a / (1 − a)): accurate verifiers count more; 50% counts zero.
  • Below 55% estimated accuracy: weight 0 (Weaver's filter step, simplified).
Part 7How this applies to us

How this applies to us

54/58
  • All checkers run on the firm's machine: code rules, agreement, a local judge model.
  • A step score shows a reviewer where an answer broke.
  • Distill the combined checker into a small model so checking stays cheap.

Weak checkers on a contract answer

55/58
Checker What it checks How it fails
Agreement Did other tries reach the same verdict? Wrong answers can cluster
LLM judge Rates the answer 0–10 Small models say yes to almost everything
Code check Does the quoted clause exist in the contract? Can't judge the reasoning
  • Different checkers fail in different ways. That is why combining helps.

Common mistakes

58/58
  • Trusting a judge that says yes to everything → measure its precision first.
  • Searching too hard against one verifier → cap samples; wrong answers start to fool it.
  • Combining verifiers with the same blind spot → they agree, so label-free weighting overrates them.
  • Reading step scores from a prompted 8B model as if they were a trained PRM → measure them against an oracle first.
  • Too few rollouts per step → each rollout moves a soft label by 1/N; use more or prefer hard labels.
  • Fitting weights on the test set → use a separate set or a label-free method.

Check your understanding

Why can majority vote fail even when the right answer was sampled?

If the right answer is rare and wrong answers cluster, the vote picks the cluster.

Why did Cobbe et al.'s accuracy fall past 400 samples?

With more samples, search finds wrong answers that fool the verifier. The default became 100 samples.

What does a process verifier give you that an outcome verifier doesn't?

A score per step, so you see where a solution broke. Lightman et al. found it also picked better: 78.2% vs 72.4%.

Why did Lightman et al. show labelers "convincing wrong-answer" solutions?

The PRM rated them highly but they are wrong, so the PRM must be wrong about some step. That made labeling about 2.6x more data-efficient.

How does Math-Shepherd label a step without people?

It finishes the solution from that step several times. The share of right final answers is the soft label; whether any is right is the hard label.

How does Weaver estimate a verifier's accuracy without labels?

From how verifiers agree with each other. Assuming they are independent given the truth, their joint vote rates pin down each one's accuracy.

Why distill Weaver into a 400M model?

The full ensemble costs 35.35 exaFLOPs per query; the cross-encoder costs 1.01 and keeps 98.2% of the performance.

CS329A Lecture 3 (Sep 29)

More tries only help if something can tell right from wrong. Verifiers score candidate answers, either the whole answer (outcome) or each step (process), and several weak verifiers combined can come close to a perfect checker.

Key terms

Verifier
Anything that scores a candidate answer: an exact checker, unit tests, a trained model, an LLM judge, or a rule.Source: Cobbe et al. 2021
Generation-verification gap
The difference between coverage (some try is right) and what your verifier actually picks.Source: Saad-Falcon et al. 2025 (Weaver)
Outcome reward model (ORM)
A verifier that judges only the final answer of a solution.Source: Lightman et al. 2023
Process reward model (PRM)
A verifier that scores each reasoning step; the solution score multiplies the step scores.Source: Lightman et al. 2023
Soft / hard step label
From a step, finish the solution N times. Soft: share that reach the right answer. Hard: 1 if any do.Source: Wang et al. 2023 (Math-Shepherd)
Weak supervision
Estimating how accurate each noisy verifier is from how they agree with each other, without true labels.Source: Saad-Falcon et al. 2025 (Weaver)
Reward hacking (verifier exploitation)
Search finds answers that score high but are wrong. More samples make it more likely.Source: Cobbe et al. 2021

The papers

Shrinking the Generation-Verification Gap with Weak Verifiers (Weaver)

Saad-Falcon, Buchanan, Chen, et al. · 2025

What
Combines many weak verifiers (33 open reward models and LLM judges, 7B-72B) into one score to pick among 100 samples from Llama 3.3 70B Instruct. Normalizes and binarizes scores, filters weak verifiers, and learns verifier weights with weak supervision (no ground-truth labels). Then distills the ensemble into a small cross-encoder.
Result
Average accuracy over MATH500, GPQA Diamond, MMLU College and MMLU Pro: 87.7% for Weaver vs 72.2% majority vote, 69.0% GPT-4o and 86.7% o3-mini; within 4.2% of the pass@100 oracle. Weighted ensembles with oracle labels beat naive averaging by up to 11.2 points. A 400M ModernBERT distillation kept 98.7% of the accuracy (98.2% in another section) while cutting verification compute by up to 99.97%. Struggles where correct answers are very rare.
Takeaway
Don't hunt for one perfect judge. Stack cheap checks that fail in different ways (rules, agreement, a judge model, reward models), weight them by estimated accuracy, and distill the result into a small model for production.

Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems

Cobbe, Kosaraju, Bavarian, Chen, Jun, Kaiser, Plappert, Tworek, Hilton, Nakano, Hesse, Schulman · 2021

What
Released GSM8K (8.5K grade-school word problems: 7.5K train, 1K test). Trained a verifier to judge whether a full solution is correct, then at test time sampled 100 solutions per problem and returned the one the verifier scored highest.
Result
Verification gave about the same boost as a 30x larger model over a fine-tuning baseline, and scaled better with more data (but did not help with small training sets). A large generator with a small verifier beat a small generator with a large verifier. For the 6B verifier, accuracy rose up to 400 completions per problem, then fell as search found wrong answers that fooled the verifier. A token-level verifier eventually beat a solution-level one.
Takeaway
A trained outcome verifier plus sampling is a cheap way to lift a small model, but cap the number of samples and watch for adversarial answers that game the verifier.

Let's Verify Step by Step

Lightman, Kosaraju, Burda, Edwards, Baker, Lee, Leike, Schulman, Sutskever, Cobbe · 2023

What
Compared an outcome reward model (judges the final answer) with a process reward model (judges each step) for picking among GPT-4 solutions on MATH. Humans labeled steps positive, negative or neutral; a solution's PRM score is the probability that every step is correct.
Result
Best-of-1,860 on a 500-problem MATH test subset: PRM 78.2%, ORM 72.4%, majority vote 69.6%. Released PRM800K: 800K step labels across 75K solutions to 12K problems. Active learning was about 2.6x more data-efficient than uniform labeling. On out-of-distribution AP and AMC problems (best-of-100) the PRM also led: aggregate 72.9% vs 63.8% ORM and 61.3% majority vote.
Takeaway
When you can, check steps, not just the result. Step scores also tell a reviewer where a solution broke. Spend labeling budget on convincing wrong answers (active learning).

Math-Shepherd: Verify and Reinforce LLMs Step-by-step without Human Annotations

Wang, Li, Shao, Xu, Dai, Li, Chen, Wu, Sui · 2023

What
Labels steps automatically: from each step, a completer model finishes the solution N times (N=8 in their data) and the step is scored by how often it reaches the right answer (soft label) or whether it ever does (hard label). Trains a process reward model on these labels and uses it for reranking and for step-by-step PPO.
Result
Step-by-step PPO lifted Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH; adding verification reached 89.1% and 43.5%. DeepSeek-67B with best-of-256 went from 88.2% (self-consistency) to 93.3% on GSM8K, and 48.1% on MATH500 when combined with self-consistency.
Takeaway
If you have final-answer checks, you can turn them into step labels with rollouts, offline. That gives a process verifier and an RL signal without paying people to label steps.

SOLVE = ("Solve this problem step by step, one step per line. Keep it short.\n"
         "On the last line write exactly: ANSWER: <whole number>\n\nProblem: {q}")

def gen(prompt, temperature=0.8, max_tokens=400):
    """One model call, returns the text. Never raises."""
    try:
        r = client.chat.completions.create(model=MODEL, messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
                                           temperature=temperature, max_tokens=max_tokens)
        return r.choices[0].message.content or ""
    except Exception as e:
        print("call failed:", e)
        return ""

def pmap(fn, items):
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
        return list(ex.map(fn, items))

2. The oracle verifier, and how we score any verifier

A verifier accepts or rejects each candidate. We score it like a classifier: - precision: of the candidates it accepted, how many were really right; - recall: of the right candidates, how many it accepted.

The oracle compares with the true answer. It is perfect by definition, and it is what Brown et al. had for code (unit tests) and proofs (Lean). For most business work you don't have one.

a02_verification.ipynb · cell 4
GOLD = {c["key"] for c in CANDS if c["ok"]}

def score_accepts(accepted: set, name: str):
    s = prf(accepted, GOLD)
    print(f"{name:26} accepts {len(accepted):2}   precision {s['precision']:.0%}   recall {s['recall']:.0%}")
    return s

oracle = score_accepts({c["key"] for c in CANDS if c["answer"] == BY_ID[c["pid"]]["answer"]}, "oracle (exact answer)")

3. An outcome verifier: an LLM judges the final answer

Cobbe et al. 2021 (Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems) trained a model to judge whole solutions, then picked the best of 100 samples. On their GSM8K dataset this gave about the same boost as a 30x bigger model. They also warn that with too many samples (beyond about 400 for their 6B verifier) accuracy dropped, because search starts finding wrong answers that fool the verifier.

We can't train here, so we prompt the model to act as the judge. One call per candidate: 24 calls.

JUDGE = ("Problem: {q}\n\nProposed solution:\n{s}\n\n"
         "Check the reasoning and arithmetic. How likely is the final answer correct, from 0 (surely wrong) to 10 (surely right)? "
         "Reply with one number only.")

def judge(c):

    m = re.search(r"\d+", r)
    return min(10, int(m.group())) / 10 if m else 0.5     # unparsable -> "don't know"

    c["judge"] = s

right_mean = sum(c["judge"] for c in CANDS if c["ok"]) / max(1, sum(c["ok"] for c in CANDS))
wrong_mean = sum(c["judge"] for c in CANDS if not c["ok"]) / max(1, sum(not c["ok"] for c in CANDS))
print(f"average judge score: right candidates {right_mean:.2f}, wrong candidates {wrong_mean:.2f}")

How to read this: small models tend to say "yes" too often, which gives high recall and poor precision. A judge that gives every candidate 9/10 can't help you pick.

4. Two cheap weak verifiers that need no model call

  • Agreement (self-consistency): what share of the other tries reached the same answer. This is majority vote turned into a score.
  • Arithmetic check: find every "a op b = c" in the text and recompute it with Python. A plain rule, but it never gets tired.
a02_verification.ipynb · cell 6
OPS = {ast.Add: op.add, ast.Sub: op.sub, ast.Mult: op.mul, ast.Div: op.truediv}

def safe_eval(expr):
    """Evaluate + - * / and brackets only. Anything else raises ValueError. No eval() on model text."""
    def ev(n):
        if isinstance(n, ast.Expression):
            return ev(n.body)
        if isinstance(n, ast.Constant) and isinstance(n.value, (int, float)):
            return n.value
        if isinstance(n, ast.BinOp) and type(n.op) in OPS:
            return OPS[type(n.op)](ev(n.left), ev(n.right))
        if isinstance(n, ast.UnaryOp) and isinstance(n.op, ast.USub):
            return -ev(n.operand)
        raise ValueError("not plain arithmetic")
    return ev(ast.parse(expr, mode="eval"))

EQ = re.compile(r"([\d.+\-*/×÷x() ]+)=\s*(-?\d+(?:\.\d+)?)")

def arithmetic_score(text):
    """Share of 'a op b = c' equations that are right. 0.5 if there are none to check."""
    ok = total = 0
    for line in (text or "").splitlines():
        line = re.sub(r"^\s*(?:step\s*)?\d+[.):]\s+", "", line, flags=re.I)         # drop "1." / "Step 2:" markers
        line = re.sub(r"(?<=\d),(?=\d{3})", "", line).replace("$", "")
        for left, right in EQ.findall(line):
            expr = left.strip().replace("×", "*").replace("÷", "/").replace("x", "*")
            if not re.search(r"\d\s*[+\-*/]\s*[\d(]", expr):
                continue
            try:
                val, want = safe_eval(expr), float(right)
            except Exception:
                continue
            total += 1
            close = abs(val - want) <= 1e-6 * max(1.0, abs(want))
            rounded = "/" in expr and want in (math.floor(val), math.ceil(val))   # "96 / 9 = 10 full bags" is fine
            ok += close or rounded
    return ok / total if total else 0.5

print(arithmetic_score("Step 1: 12 * 5 = 60\nStep 2: 60 - 7 = 53"), arithmetic_score("12 x 5 = 70"), arithmetic_score("no maths here"))

for c in CANDS:
    others = [o["answer"] for o in CANDS if o["pid"] == c["pid"] and o["key"] != c["key"]]
    c["agree"] = sum(a == c["answer"] for a in others) / len(others) if c["answer"] is not None and others else 0.0
    c["arith"] = arithmetic_score(c["text"])
agree_acc = score_accepts({c["key"] for c in CANDS if c["agree"] >= 0.5}, "agreement (>= half agree)")
arith_acc = score_accepts({c["key"] for c in CANDS if c["arith"] >= 0.75}, "arithmetic check (>= 75%)")

5. A process verifier: score each step (PRM-style)

Lightman et al. 2023 (Let's Verify Step by Step) compared an outcome reward model (ORM: judges only the end result) with a process reward model (PRM: judges every step). People labeled 800K steps (the PRM800K dataset). Picking the best of 1,860 GPT-4 solutions on a 500-problem MATH test set, the PRM got 78.2%, the ORM 72.4%, and majority vote 69.6%. A solution's PRM score is the chance that every step is right: multiply the step scores.

We prompt our model to score each numbered step, on 6 candidates (right and wrong mixed). That is 6 calls.

a02_verification.ipynb · cell 7
class StepScores(BaseModel):
    scores: list[int]

def steps_of(text, max_steps=8):
    lines = [l.strip() for l in (text or "").splitlines() if l.strip()]
    if len(lines) <= max_steps:
        return lines
    size = math.ceil(len(lines) / max_steps)
    return [" ".join(lines[i:i + size]) for i in range(0, len(lines), size)]

PRM_PROMPT = ("Problem: {q}\n\nA solution, split into numbered steps:\n{steps}\n\n"
              "For EACH step, give a score from 0 (this step is wrong) to 10 (this step is surely correct). "
              "Judge the step itself: its logic and arithmetic. Return one score per step, in order.")

def prm(c):
    st = steps_of(c["text"])
    body = "\n".join(f"{i + 1}. {s}" for i, s in enumerate(st))
    try:
        r = structured(PRM_PROMPT.format(q=BY_ID[c["pid"]]["question"], steps=body), StepScores, max_tokens=200)
        sc = [min(10, max(0, int(x))) / 10 for x in r.scores]
    except Exception as e:
        print("PRM call failed:", e)
        sc = []
    sc = (sc + [0.5] * len(st))[:len(st)]          # pad or cut to one score per step
    return st, sc

right = [c for c in CANDS if c["ok"]][:3]
wrong = [c for c in CANDS if not c["ok"]][:6 - len(right)]
PRM_SET = right + wrong
for c, (st, sc) in zip(PRM_SET, pmap(prm, PRM_SET)):
    c["steps"], c["step_scores"] = st, sc
    c["prm_prod"] = math.prod(sc) if sc else 0.0
    c["prm_min"] = min(sc) if sc else 0.0
    first_bad = next((i + 1 for i, s in enumerate(sc) if s < 0.5), None)
    print(f"{c['key']:16} right={c['ok']!s:5}  outcome judge {c['judge']:.1f}  PRM product {c['prm_prod']:.2f}  "
          f"min {c['prm_min']:.1f}  first flagged step: {first_bad}")

Why steps help: a wrong final answer usually comes from one bad step. A step score tells you where it broke, which is useful for a human reviewer, for retrying from that step, and as a training signal.

6. Step labels without humans (Math-Shepherd)

People labeling 800K steps is expensive. Wang et al. 2023 (Math-Shepherd) label steps automatically: cut the solution after step i, let a model finish it N times, and check the final answers. - Soft label: share of finishes that reach the right answer. - Hard label: 1 if any finish reaches the right answer.

They built their dataset with N = 8 finishes per step, and used it to train a PRM and to guide RL. We use N = 3 on 3 cut points of one right and one wrong candidate: 18 calls.

a02_verification.ipynb · cell 8
CONTINUE = ("Problem: {q}\n\nHere is the start of a solution:\n{prefix}\n\n"
            "Continue from exactly where it stops. Do not start over. "
            "On the last line write exactly: ANSWER: <whole number>")
ROLLOUTS = 3

def cut_points(n_steps):
    return sorted({1, max(1, n_steps // 2), max(1, n_steps - 1)})

def shepherd(c):
    p = BY_ID[c["pid"]]
    rows = []
    for i in cut_points(len(c["steps"])):
        prefix = "\n".join(c["steps"][:i])
        outs = pmap(lambda _: gen(CONTINUE.format(q=p["question"], prefix=prefix)), range(ROLLOUTS))
        hits = sum(extract_int(o) == p["answer"] for o in outs)
        rows.append({"after_step": i, "soft": hits / ROLLOUTS, "hard": int(hits > 0),
                     "prm": c["step_scores"][i - 1] if i - 1 < len(c["step_scores"]) else None})
    return rows

SHEP = {}
for c in [x for x in PRM_SET if x["ok"]][:1] + [x for x in PRM_SET if not x["ok"]][:1]:
    SHEP[c["key"]] = (c["ok"], shepherd(c))
    print(f"\n{c['key']} (candidate is {'right' if c['ok'] else 'wrong'})")
    for r in SHEP[c["key"]][1]:
        print(f"  after step {r['after_step']}: soft {r['soft']:.2f}  hard {r['hard']}  | prompted PRM said {r['prm']}")

How to read this: on a wrong solution, the soft label should drop after the step where it went wrong. Compare that with the step the prompted PRM flagged. Math-Shepherd reports that training on these labels lifted Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K with step-by-step RL, and to 89.1% when also used as a verifier. The catch: rollouts cost many calls per step, so this is an offline, data-making tool.

7. Combine weak verifiers (Weaver)

Saad-Falcon et al. 2025 (Shrinking the Generation-Verification Gap with Weak Verifiers, "Weaver") start from many imperfect verifiers: reward models and LLM judges. Each one alone is weak. Their recipe: 1. turn each verifier's score into a yes/no vote (they normalize and binarize); 2. estimate how accurate each verifier is without labels, from how its votes agree with the others (weak supervision); 3. drop verifiers that look no better than chance, and weight the rest by accuracy.

With Llama 3.3 70B as the generator and 100 samples per question, Weaver averaged 87.7% across MATH500, GPQA Diamond, MMLU College and MMLU Pro, against 72.2% for majority vote and 86.7% for o3-mini.

Our tiny version uses 3 verifiers we already have: judge, agreement, arithmetic. No new calls.

a02_verification.ipynb · cell 9
VERIFIERS = {"judge": lambda c: c["judge"] >= 0.7, "agree": lambda c: c["agree"] >= 0.5, "arith": lambda c: c["arith"] >= 0.75}
VOTES = {c["key"]: {v: int(f(c)) for v, f in VERIFIERS.items()} for c in CANDS}

def label_free_accuracy(name):
    """How well this verifier agrees with the average vote of the OTHER verifiers. No labels used.

    Per candidate: 1 if it matches all the others, 0 if it matches none, 0.5 on a split. Then average.
    A verifier that says "yes" to everything only scores as well as the others say "yes".
    """
    scores = []
    for votes in VOTES.values():
        others = [x for v, x in votes.items() if v != name]
        if others:
            scores.append(1 - abs(votes[name] - sum(others) / len(others)))
    return sum(scores) / len(scores) if scores else 0.5

def true_accuracy(name):
    return sum(VOTES[c["key"]][name] == int(c["ok"]) for c in CANDS) / len(CANDS)

def weights(acc_fn, min_acc=0.55):
    out = {}
    for v in VERIFIERS:
        a = min(0.95, max(0.05, acc_fn(v)))
        out[v] = math.log(a / (1 - a)) if a >= min_acc else 0.0     # filter: weak-looking verifiers get no say
    if not any(out.values()):                                         # everything filtered: fall back to equal votes
        print("note: no verifier passed the filter, using equal weights")
        out = {v: 1.0 for v in VERIFIERS}
    return out

W_FREE, W_LABELS = weights(label_free_accuracy), weights(true_accuracy)
for v in VERIFIERS:
    print(f"{v:6} accuracy estimated without labels {label_free_accuracy(v):.0%}  true {true_accuracy(v):.0%}  "
          f"-> weight {W_FREE[v]:+.2f} (with labels {W_LABELS[v]:+.2f})")

def combined(c, w):
    return sum(w[v] * (2 * VOTES[c["key"]][v] - 1) for v in VERIFIERS)

# "with labels" uses the true answers of these same candidates, so it is a cheating upper bound,
# shown only to see how close the label-free weights get.
weaver_acc = score_accepts({c["key"] for c in CANDS if combined(c, W_FREE) > 0}, "Weaver-lite (no labels)")
naive_acc = score_accepts({c["key"] for c in CANDS if sum(VOTES[c["key"]].values()) >= 2}, "naive vote (2 of 3)")

8. What matters in the end: did we pick the right answer?

For each problem, keep the candidate with the highest score (ties go to the earlier try). Compare pickers. "Oracle" is the share of problems where some try was right: the ceiling.

a02_verification.ipynb · cell 10
def pick_accuracy(score_fn):
    right = 0
    for p in PROBS:
        cs = [c for c in CANDS if c["pid"] == p["id"]]
        best = max(cs, key=score_fn)            # max keeps the first of equal scores
        right += best["ok"]
    return right / len(PROBS)

PICKERS = {
    "first try (no verifier)": lambda c: 0,
    "majority vote": lambda c: c["agree"],
    "LLM judge": lambda c: c["judge"],
    "arithmetic check": lambda c: c["arith"],
    "Weaver-lite (no labels)": lambda c: (combined(c, W_FREE), c["agree"]),
    "weighted with labels": lambda c: (combined(c, W_LABELS), c["agree"]),
    "oracle": lambda c: c["ok"],
}
RESULTS = {name: pick_accuracy(fn) for name, fn in PICKERS.items()}
for name, acc in RESULTS.items():
    print(f"{name:26} {acc:.0%}")

How to read this: with 6 problems one flip is 17 points, so treat this as a demo of the method, not a result. The lessons from the papers are: (1) a verifier's precision matters more than its recall when you pick one answer, (2) several weak, different verifiers beat one, and (3) Weaver distilled its ensemble into a 400M model that kept about 98% of the accuracy at a tiny fraction of the verification compute, so the combination can be cheap in production.

Traps
  • Trusting an LLM judge that says yes to almost everything. High recall with low precision can't help you pick one answer.
  • Searching too hard against one verifier. Cobbe et al. saw accuracy fall past 400 samples as wrong answers started fooling the verifier.
  • Combining verifiers that share the same blind spot (two prompts to the same model). They agree with each other, so label-free weighting overrates them.
  • Reading step scores from a prompted 8B model as if they were a trained PRM. They are a weak signal; measure them against an oracle first.
  • Using rollout-based step labels with too few rollouts. Each rollout moves a soft label by 1/N, so small N gives noisy labels.
  • Fitting verifier weights on your test set and reporting the result. Use a separate set, or a label-free method.

Check yourself

Why can majority vote fail even when the right answer was sampled?

If the right answer is rare (sampled once in many tries) and wrong answers cluster, the vote picks the cluster. Brown et al. note correct answers can have 1% or lower probability.

What does a process verifier give you that an outcome verifier doesn't?

A score per step, so you see where a solution broke. Lightman et al. found it also picked better: 78.2% vs 72.4% for outcome supervision on their MATH subset.

How does Math-Shepherd label a step without people?

It finishes the solution from that step several times and checks the final answers: the share that are right is the soft label; whether any is right is the hard label.
