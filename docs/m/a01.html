Test-time compute scaling

Open in Colab
22 min readSlides first, then the deeper material

Let a model try many times or revise its work, then pick well. More tries only pay off if you can spot the right one.

Learning objective

1/69
  • Learn how to buy accuracy at answer time: how coverage grows with tries, why it follows a power law, how to stack sampling, ranking, fusing and checking, and how to spend compute by question difficulty.
Part 1Why this lecture exists

Why test-time compute?

2/69
  • Test-time compute: extra work spent when answering (more tries, revisions, search) instead of when training.
  • No training needed. Same model, more tries.
  • Two ways to spend it:
    • Parallel: many independent tries, then pick one
    • Sequential: one try, then revise it, then revise again
  • The catch: something must tell the right try from the wrong ones.

Two problems: coverage and precision

3/69
  • Brown et al. split repeated sampling into two steps (Figure 1):
    • Step 1: generate many candidate answers at temperature above 0.
    • Step 2: a verifier picks a final answer (unit tests, proof checker, majority vote).
  • Coverage: can we generate a correct answer at all?
  • Precision: can we find the correct answer among the samples?
  • Both are needed. With unlimited samples, any model that gives every answer some chance reaches perfect coverage.

Four papers, one story

4/69
Paper Question Short answer
Large Language Monkeys (Brown et al. 2024) Does sampling more help? Coverage grows over 4 orders of magnitude; picking stalls
Power Laws (Schaeffer et al. 2025) Why that curve shape? A heavy tail of very hard problems
Archon (Saad-Falcon et al. 2024) How to combine tricks? Stack them in layers; search the stack
Compute-optimal (Snell et al. 2024) How to spend a fixed budget? It depends on question difficulty
Part 2Large Language Monkeys (Brown et al. 2024)

The problem

5/69
  • Training compute has been scaled hard: bigger models, more data.
  • At answer time, we usually give the model one attempt.
  • Idea: treat inference compute as its own scaling axis.
  • Method: the simplest one. Sample the same prompt many times, independently, same settings.
  • Title from the "infinite monkey theorem".

Repeated sampling and coverage

6/69
  • Repeated sampling: ask the same question many times at temperature above 0, so the tries differ.
  • Temperature 0: every try is the same. More tries add nothing.
  • Coverage (pass@k): share of problems where at least one of k tries is right.
  • Coverage is an upper bound. It assumes a perfect checker.
  • For GSM8K and MATH, coverage means an oracle verifier checks the final answer.

The pass@k formula

8/69
  • pass@k = mean over problems of [ 1 − C(N − C_i, k) / C(N, k) ] (Equation 1)
    • N: samples drawn per problem
    • C_i: how many of them were right on problem i
    • C(a, b): "a choose b", the number of ways to pick b items from a
    • The fraction: chance that k tries picked from the N are all wrong
  • Unbiased estimator from Chen et al. (Codex paper); lower variance than drawing only k samples.

Coverage grows on every task (Figure 2)

9/69
  • Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Llama-3-70B-Instruct: 10,000 samples per problem.
  • SWE-bench Lite with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct + Moatless Tools (agent framework): up to 250 attempts.
    • One attempt = one full multi-turn trajectory.
  • SWE-bench Lite: 15.9% with 1 sample → 56% with 250.
    • Single-attempt state of the art then: 43% (CodeStory Aide, GPT-4o + Claude 3.5 Sonnet).
  • With one try, GPT-4o beats the Llama and DeepSeek models on every task. With more tries, all three weaker models pass it.

Across sizes and families (Figure 3)

10/69
  • Models: Llama 3 (8B, 8B-Instruct, 70B-Instruct), Gemma (2B, 7B), Pythia (70M to 12B, eight models).
  • Small models show some of the sharpest gains:
    • Gemma-2B, CodeContests: 0.02% pass@1 → 7.1% pass@10k (over 300x)
    • Pythia-160M, MATH: 0.27% pass@1 → 57% pass@10k
  • Exception: every Pythia model got 0% on CodeContests, even with 10,000 samples.
    • Authors' guess: Pythia saw less code in training.

Cost in FLOPs (Section 2.3)

11/69
  • FLOPs per token ≈ 2 × (parameters + 2 × layers × token dim × context length)
    • First term: the matrix multiplies through the weights
    • Second term: attention over the context so far
  • Total = FLOPs over prompt tokens + FLOPs over decoded tokens × number of completions.
  • Figure 4, fixed FLOP budget:
    • MiniF2F, GSM8K, MATH: the 8B model always gets higher coverage than 70B.
    • CodeContests: the 70B model is almost always more cost-effective.
  • Caveat: FLOPs ignore batching and other system effects.

An inference scaling law (Section 3.1)

13/69
  • log(coverage) ≈ a · k^b, so coverage ≈ exp(a · k^b) (Equations 2–3)
    • k: number of samples
    • a, b: fitted numbers, both negative here
    • Called an "exponentiated power law"
  • Borrowed from the GPT-4 report, which fit pass rate vs training compute the same way.
  • Not as exact as training scaling laws, "most strikingly on MiniF2F-MATH".

Same family, same curve shape (Figure 6)

15/69
  • On a log-k axis, coverage curves look like S-curves.
  • Within one family (Llama-3, Gemma, Pythia), curves have the same slope, shifted left or right.
  • Test: shift every curve so it passes through (1, c), where c = best pass@1 in the family. The curves overlap.
  • Meaning: going from coverage c to c′ takes about the same multiple of samples for every model in a family.

Picking is the hard part (Figure 7)

16/69
  • Only GSM8K and MATH lack automatic checkers. Three pickers tested:
    • Majority vote: most common final answer
    • Reward model + best-of-N: highest score from a reward model (ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1)
    • Reward model + majority vote: votes weighted by reward score
  • All three rise, then plateau around 100 samples. Coverage keeps rising past 95%.

Coverage vs pickers on MATH

17/69

Llama-3-8B-Instruct: 82.9% at 100 samples → 98.44% at 10,000 Best gain over the same range: 40.50% → 41.41%
  • Generation-verification gap: the model could produce the right answer, but the picker can't find it.
  • The gap grows as samples grow.
  • Module a02 is about closing this gap.

Why majority vote stalls (Figure 8)

18/69
  • Figure 8: one bar per problem, height = share of 10,000 samples that are right.
  • Many problems have correct answers sampled rarely: 1% or lower.
  • A rare right answer never becomes the most common answer.
  • So majority vote can't use it, no matter how many samples you add.
  • Verifiers must "find the needle in the haystack".

Software checkers are imperfect too (Section 4.2)

20/69
  • Flaky tests: 11.3% of SWE-bench Lite problems have test suites that give different results on the same solution.
    • Some even fail the dataset's own correct fix.
    • Two issues use Python sets (unordered); some model fixes pass only on "lucky" runs.
  • False negatives in CodeContests: of 122 test problems with Python3 solutions, 35 have "correct" solutions that fail the tests.
    • Causes: several valid outputs but one expected; mutated inputs that break the problem's rules.

Limitations and next steps (Section 5)

21/69
  • Only the simplest setup: independent tries, same prompt, same settings.
  • Diversity: temperature is the only source of variety. AlphaCode adds metadata tags per sample.
  • Multi-turn: no execution feedback, even where checkers exist.
  • Learning from earlier attempts: tries are fully isolated.
  • Systems: repeated sampling can use big batches and shared-prompt attention tricks, so it costs less than many chatbot API calls.
Part 3How Do Large Language Monkeys Get Their Power (Laws)? (Schaeffer et al. 2025)

The puzzle

22/69
  • Brown et al.: −log(average pass@k) ≈ a · k^(−b). A power law in k (Figure 1, top).
  • Hughes et al. (Best-of-N Jailbreaking) saw the same shape for jailbreak success rate on frontier models (Figure 1, bottom).
  • But simple math says each single problem should improve exponentially in k, not as a power law.
  • Question: where does the aggregate power law come from?

One problem: exponential (Equations 5–9)

23/69
  • pass_i@k = 1 − (1 − pass_i@1)^k
    • pass_i@1: chance one try solves problem i
    • (1 − pass_i@1)^k: chance all k independent tries fail
  • For large k: −log(pass_i@k) ≈ (1 − pass_i@1)^k
  • That falls exponentially fast in k.
  • Confirmed in Figure 3: Pythia on 128 MATH problems; frontier models on 159 HarmBench prompts.
    • Every single problem falls exponentially. Only the average (black line) is a power law.

Many problems: an average of exponentials (Equation 10)

24/69
  • pass_D@k = 1 − ∫ (1 − p)^k · p_D(p) dp
    • p: one problem's single-try success rate
    • p_D(p): how common problems with that rate are in the benchmark (the distribution D)
    • The integral: average failure chance over all problems
  • Known result: a well-weighted sum of exponentials can make a power law.
  • So the answer must lie in the shape of p_D near p = 0.

The theorem (Theorems 3.1 and 3.2)

25/69
  • If p_D(p) ≈ C · p^(b−1) near p = 0, then −log(pass_D@k) ≈ C · Γ(b) · k^(−b) for large k.
    • C: how many hard problems there are
    • b: how fast the density thins out near 0; also the power-law exponent
    • Γ(b): the gamma function, just a constant here
  • The reverse also holds (under mild conditions). The tail near 0 is necessary and sufficient.
  • Intuition: a few near-unsolvable problems stay unsolved for many tries and drag the average.

Which distributions give a power law

26/69
Distribution of single-try success −log(pass_D@k) scales like
Uniform(0, β ≤ 1) k^(−1)
Beta(α, β) k^(−α)
Kumaraswamy(α, β) k^(−α)
Continuous Bernoulli (λ < 1/2) k^(−1)
  • Beta and Kumaraswamy: flexible shapes on [0, 1]; α controls the tail near 0.
  • Smaller α → more near-zero problems → flatter line → each 10x more samples helps less.

The real data has heavy left tails (Figure 4)

27/69
  • Histograms of pass_i@1 for Pythia (128 MATH problems) and frontier models (159 HarmBench prompts).
  • Well fit by a scaled 3-parameter Kumaraswamy distribution.
    • The scale parameter matters: most success rates top out near 0.01 or 0.1, not 1.
  • Exception: Llama 3 8B IT was jailbroken on every prompt within the budget. No heavy tail.
  • Matching result: Llama 3 8B IT showed no aggregate power law (Section 4).

A better way to forecast (Figure 5)

28/69
Least squares (standard) Distributional (new)
Estimate pass_i@k at several k Estimate pass_i@1 per problem
Average over problems Fit a distribution to those rates
Fit a line in log-log space Simulate pass_D@k at any k, then fit
  • Obstacle: problems with success below 1 / (samples drawn) look like 0.
  • Fix: discretize the distribution at that resolution; match how many problems fall in the "too rare to see" bucket.

Forecasting results (Figures 6–7)

29/69
  • With all data, both estimators agree on the exponent (Figure 6).
  • Backtest on synthetic data with a known true exponent, using fewer problems and fewer samples (Figure 7).
  • Distributional estimator: about an order of magnitude lower relative error, |b̂ − b| / b.
  • Authors equate that to roughly 2–4 orders of magnitude less inference compute.
  • Works even when the fitted distribution is the wrong family.

Why the tails exist, and a bigger idea (Section 7)

30/69
  • Conjecture 1: benchmark design. Problems are chosen to span difficulty, not too easy, not too hard.
  • Conjecture 2: selection bias. Power laws get noticed and published.
  • Bigger idea: pretraining loss may also be a sum of terms decaying at different rates.
    • Only at large compute does the slowest term win and a clean power law appear.
    • They call hidden terms "the dark matter of neural scaling laws".
Part 4Archon (Saad-Falcon et al. 2024)

The problem

31/69
  • Inference-time techniques: sampling, ranking, fusing, critiquing, verifying.
  • No single best way to combine them. Three gaps:
    • Which technique helps which task?
    • How do techniques interact (more models, or more samples per model)?
    • The design space is huge; searching it by hand is slow.
  • Archon: a modular framework plus automatic search over stacks of techniques.

The building blocks (Section 3.1)

32/69
Component Input → output
Generator Prompt → candidate answers (one or many models, many samples)
Fuser Prompt + candidates → one or more merged, better answers
Ranker Prompt + candidates → ranked list, keep top-K
Critic Prompt + candidates → strengths and weaknesses of each
Verifier Prompt + candidate → reasoning, then [Correct] or [Incorrect]
Unit Test Generator / Evaluator Prompt → 5–10 test statements; then rank candidates by tests passed

What each block was worth

33/69
  • Fuser: helped on every benchmark, 8.9% on average.
    • MixEval: 1–2 points from more fuser layers; MT Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0: 10 to 15 points with 3–4 layers.
  • Ranker: 10.8% over random selection on MT Bench and Arena-Hard-Auto, within 2.7% of oracle selection.
  • Critic: +11.5 percentage points on average, added to a 10-model 70B+ ensemble with a Fuser.
  • Verifier: 8.4% on MixEval, MixEval Hard and MATH.
    • Verifier alone after generation lagged ensemble + fuser by 1.5%. Best combined with other blocks.

Unit tests and more samples on code

34/69
  • Unit Test Generator + Evaluator: 7.4% boost on benchmarks needing more verification steps (Table 5).
  • With 70B+ generators + unit tests only: lagged ensemble + fuser by 3.1% on Arena-Hard-Auto and MixEval.
  • CodeContests with more samples + unit tests: Pass@1 17.9% → 29.3%, a 56% boost.
  • GPT-4o on CodeContests: 40 → 58 of 140 questions (44.3%) with unit tests and more samples (Section 4.3).

Four trends (Section 3.2)

35/69
  • T1: more samples and more ensemble models help: 9.3% and 18.5%.
  • T2: more layers help, e.g. always end with a single Fuser.
  • T3: more kinds of techniques help; critics and rankers before fusers work well.
  • T4: on reasoning, Verifier and unit tests next to the Fuser filter out flawed answers.
  • Figure 4: adding layers of critique and fusion gave an 18.8% boost on average.

Built like a neural network

36/69
  • Layers of LLM components; components in one layer run in parallel.
  • Every layer takes and returns a list of strings (prompt or candidate answers).
    • Generator, Fuser: turn a list into a new list.
    • Ranker, Verifier: filter the list, like a non-linearity.
  • No weights are learned. It runs off the shelf.
  • If the last layer returns a list, the first string is the answer.

Rules for construction

37/69
  • One component type per layer.
  • Generators only in the first layer.
  • A Critic must come before a Ranker or a Fuser.
  • Ranker, Critic, Verifier, Unit Test layers: anywhere but the first, alone in their layer.
  • Fusers: anywhere but the first; several per layer allowed.
  • Unit Test Generator, then Unit Test Evaluator, in consecutive layers.

An example stack (Figure 3)

38/69
10 generators (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 405B, ...)
→ critic (Qwen2-72B) → ranker (Claude 3.5 Sonnet)
→ 6 fusers → verifier (GPT-4o) → 1 fuser (Llama 3.1 405B) → answer
  • Wide at the start, narrowing at the end.
  • Best general all-source stack found: 10 generators, then four layers of critique and fusion with 8, 6, 4 fusers ("funneling").

The search space (Section 3.3)

39/69
  • Six knobs:
    • Top-K generator models: 1 to 10
    • Samples per generator: 1 to 5 (CodeContests: 1, 10, 100, 500, 1000)
    • Fusion layers: 1 to 4; last layer has one Fuser
    • Fusers per layer: 2 to 10, in steps of 2
    • Critic and Ranker layers before each fuser layer
    • Evaluation layer: Verifier, unit tests, or neither, before the last Fuser
  • 9,576 valid configurations in total.

How the search works

40/69
  • Inputs: target benchmarks, inference call budget, available LLMs, available techniques.
  • 20% of each dataset guides the search; results reported on the other 80%.
  • Three methods: random, greedy (one knob at a time), Bayesian optimization (a model of "which settings score well" picks the next stack to try).
  • Bayesian optimization found the best stack in 96.0% of searches.
    • 88.5% fewer evaluations than greedy, 90.4% fewer than random.
  • Under 20 calls of budget, Bayesian optimization is less effective; greedy can match it.

By task (Section 4.3)

44/69
  • Instruction following: open Archon beats open baselines by 10.5%; closed Archon beats closed by 14.6%.
  • Reasoning: smaller gains overall, but per-subtask stacks add 3.7 (MixEval) and 8.9 (MixEval-Hard) points.
  • Coding: ensembling, fusion and ranking help little; one or two models are far ahead of the rest.
    • What helps code: more samples plus unit tests.
  • Math stacks: broad generation, then quickly narrow to one answer.

Limits (Section 4.4)

45/69
  • Works best with 70B+ models.
    • 7B-only stacks: +7.5% over the best single 7B model; good at ranking, weak at critique and fusion.
  • Latency and cost: about 5x the time and money of one API call.
  • Future: distill the stack into a smaller model for speed.
Part 5Scaling Test-Time Compute Optimally (Snell et al. 2024)

The question

46/69
  • With a fixed but non-trivial budget at answer time, how much can a model improve on a hard prompt?
  • And can that replace a bigger pretrained model?
  • Prior work was mixed: some gains, some failures on math reasoning.
  • Setting: MATH (12k train, 500 test, the Lightman et al. split), PaLM 2-S* base model.
  • Revision and verifier models were fine-tuned; prompting alone did not give these skills.

Two knobs: proposer and verifier (Section 2)

47/69
Change the proposer (input side) Change the verifier (output side)
Make the model's own answers better Sample many answers, then select
E.g. revise its previous attempts in context E.g. best-of-N, or tree search on a process reward model
"Sequential" "Parallel" or search
  • Proposal distribution: what answers the model tends to write for this prompt.

Compute-optimal strategy (Equation 1)

48/69
  • θ(q, N) = argmax over θ of E[ 1{y = y(q)} ], with y drawn from Target(θ, N, q)
    • q: the prompt
    • N: compute budget
    • θ: settings (e.g. how much sequential vs parallel, which search)
    • 1{y = y*}: 1 if the answer is right
  • In words: for each prompt and budget, pick the settings with the best expected accuracy.
  • They approximate it by choosing settings per difficulty level.

Measuring difficulty (Section 3.2)

49/69
  • Difficulty is relative to the model, not a human label.
  • Oracle difficulty: pass@1 from 2048 samples per question, binned into 5 quantiles.
    • More predictive than MATH's hand-labeled levels.
  • Model-predicted difficulty: same binning, but on the verifier's average score. No true answers needed.
  • Cost of predicting difficulty is not counted in their results.
  • Two-fold cross-validation: pick the strategy on one half, score it on the other.

Training a verifier for search (Section 5.1)

50/69
  • PRM (process reward model): scores each step of a solution.
  • PRM800K human labels didn't work for them: easy to exploit, likely GPT-4 vs PaLM 2 mismatch.
  • Instead: Math-Shepherd-style labels from Monte Carlo rollouts (finish from each step, check answers).
  • PRM beat an outcome reward model (ORM) throughout, so all search uses the PRM.
  • Step aggregation: use the PRM score at the last step (beat product and minimum).
  • Answer aggregation: "best-of-N weighted" = sum verifier scores over all samples with the same final answer.

Three search methods (Figure 2)

51/69
Method How it works Cost
Best-of-N weighted N full answers, pick best by PRM N
Beam search N partial answers per step; keep top N/M; expand each M ways; repeat (max 40 rounds) N
Lookahead search Beam search, but score a step by rolling out k more steps (temperature 0) N × (k + 1)
  • Beam search = lookahead with k = 0.
  • Lookahead is MCTS (Monte Carlo tree search) without the random exploration parts.

Which search wins (Figure 3, left)

52/69
  • Low budgets: beam search clearly beats best-of-N.
  • High budgets: the gain shrinks; beam search often falls below best-of-N.
  • Lookahead usually loses at equal budget (extra rollout cost).
  • Cause: search exploits the PRM.
    • Repetitive, low-information steps at the end of solutions
    • Overly short solutions of just 1–2 steps

Search by difficulty (Figure 3, right)

53/69
Difficulty bins Beam search vs best-of-N (budgets 4, 16, 64, 256)
1–2 (easy) Beam gets worse as budget grows: over-optimizes the verifier
3–4 (medium) Beam consistently better
5 (hardest) No method makes much progress
  • Easy: the verifier is mostly right already; more search amplifies its flaws.
  • Harder: the model rarely samples the right answer; search guides it there.

Compute-optimal search (Figure 4)

54/69
  • Pick the best search setting per difficulty bin.
  • Low budgets: nearly beats PRM best-of-N with up to 4x less compute (16 vs 64 generations).
  • High budgets: the edge shrinks with predicted difficulty; continues with oracle difficulty.
  • Oracle and predicted difficulty curves largely overlap.

Revision models (Section 6.1)

55/69

  • Training data: 64 parallel samples per question at higher temperature.
    • Pair each correct answer with 0–4 incorrect answers in context (number chosen at random).
    • Pick incorrect answers close to the correct one by character edit distance.
  • At test time: keep the last 4 attempts in context, so chains can run longer.
  • Problem: about 38% of correct answers got changed back to incorrect.

Scoring majority vote in code

The power-law check in code

63/69
def agg_fail(k, a=ALPHA, b=BETA):
    """Mean over problems of (1-p)^k when p ~ Beta(a, b). Exact: B(a, b+k) / B(a, b)."""
    lb = lambda x, y: math.lgamma(x) + math.lgamma(y) - math.lgamma(x + y)
    return math.exp(lb(a, b + k) - lb(a, b))
  • agg_fail(k): average chance that all k tries fail, when success rates follow Beta(a, b).
  • lb: log of the Beta function, built from log-gamma so big k doesn't overflow.
  • Return: the exact average of (1 − p)^k.
  • The notebook fits the slope for k ≥ 100 and checks it matches −ALPHA (Schaeffer et al.'s Beta row).

How this applies to us

65/69
  • A small local model is cheap to run many times on the firm's own machine.
  • Contract checks often have a code checker: does the quote exist? Is the cap at most 12 months of fees?
  • Where nothing can check the answer, more tries mostly add cost. Build the checker first.

  • The tries are cheap. The checker is what turns coverage into a right answer.
  • One right try is enough, if code can recognize it.

Plan a sampling budget before you spend

67/69
  • Sample a small batch (say 6 tries) per contract question on a test set.
  • Look at per-question success rates (Schaeffer et al.): many near-zero questions → brute force will be slow.
  • Route by difficulty (Snell et al.): questions where tries agree get one try plus a revision; split answers get more parallel tries.
  • Questions no try solves: a bigger local model, or a person.

Stack the steps as swappable parts

68/69
  • Archon's lesson: generate, rank, fuse, verify are separate steps you can turn on and off.
  • On the firm's machine, each step is just another local model call.
  • Let a small labeled set of contracts choose the stack per task (cap check vs governing law vs renewal).
  • Count tokens, not calls: a fuser reads every candidate.
Part 8Wrap-up

Common mistakes

69/69
  • Reporting coverage as accuracy → report what your real picker gets.
  • Adding samples without a checker → build verification first; voting stalls near 100 tries.
  • Sampling at temperature 0 → every try is the same; use a temperature above 0.
  • Counting calls, not tokens → a fuser or revision call reads all candidates; count tokens.
  • Assuming more tries always beat a bigger model → Snell et al. found pretraining wins on the hardest questions and at high traffic.
  • Tuning the strategy on the set you report on → hold out data for the final score.

Check your understanding

Coverage at 100 tries is 90%, majority vote at 100 tries is 45%. What is missing?

A good verifier. The model can produce the right answer, but the picker can't find it: the generation-verification gap.

Why does majority vote stop improving when coverage keeps rising?

New coverage comes from rare right answers (1% of samples or less). A rare answer never becomes the most common one, so the vote ignores it.

Why does the average success curve follow a power law when each single problem improves exponentially?

A heavy tail of very hard problems with near-zero success per try. Each is solved exponentially fast, but there are enough of them to slow the average to a power law.

On SWE-bench Lite, why did 5 DeepSeek tries beat one GPT-4o try?

Each DeepSeek attempt cost $0.0072 vs $0.13, and the unit tests pick the right attempt. 5 tries solved 29.62% for $10.8; GPT-4o solved 24.00% for $39.

In Archon, why must a Critic come before a Ranker or Fuser?

The Critic writes strengths and weaknesses of each candidate. Only a later Ranker or Fuser can use that text.

Why did beam search hurt on easy questions in Snell et al.?

The verifier is already mostly right on easy questions. Strong search then exploits its flaws, finding answers it scores high but that are wrong.

For an easy question, should you spend 4 calls on 4 parallel tries or 1 try plus 3 revisions?

Snell et al. found revision best on easy questions. They used a fine-tuned revision model, so test it on your own task.

Key terms, papers, evidence, the full notebook code and worked solutions.

CS329A Lecture 2 (Sep 26)

You can buy accuracy at answer time instead of training time: let the model try many times, or revise its own work, and keep a good answer. The gain is only real if something can tell which try is right, and the best way to spend the compute depends on how hard the question is.

Key terms

Test-time compute
Extra computation spent when answering (more samples, revisions, search) instead of when training.Source: Snell et al. 2024
Coverage (pass@k)
Share of problems where at least one of k tries is right. An upper bound on what any picker can reach.Source: Brown et al. 2024
Repeated sampling
Asking the same question many times at a temperature above 0 so the tries differ.Source: Brown et al. 2024
Majority vote (self-consistency)
Keep the answer that the most tries agree on. Needs no checker, but plateaus.Source: Brown et al. 2024
Fuser / Ranker
A model call that merges several candidates into one answer / that orders candidates and keeps the best.Source: Saad-Falcon et al. 2024 (Archon)

The papers

Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute with Repeated Sampling

Brown, Juravsky, Ehrlich, Clark, Le, Ré, Mirhoseini · 2024

What
Sampled the same model up to 10,000 times per problem on GSM8K, MATH, MiniF2F, CodeContests and SWE-bench Lite. Measured coverage (share of problems where any sample is right) and fit it with an exponentiated power law in the number of samples.
Result
Coverage grew with samples over four orders of magnitude. SWE-bench Lite with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct: 15.9% with one sample to 56% with 250 (single-sample state of the art was 43%). 5 DeepSeek tries solved 29.62% for $10.8 vs 24.00% for $39 with one GPT-4o try. On MATH with Llama-3-8B-Instruct, coverage went from 82.9% (100 samples) to 98.44% (10,000), but majority vote and reward models plateaued around 100 samples (best gain only 40.50% to 41.41%).
Takeaway
If your task has a cheap automatic checker (tests, a solver, a schema plus business rules), many cheap tries from a small local model can beat one expensive call. Without a checker, invest in verification before you invest in more samples.

Archon: An Architecture Search Framework for Inference-Time Techniques

Saad-Falcon, Gamarra Lafuente, Natarajan, Maru, Todorov, Guha, Buchanan, Chen, Guha, Ré, Mirhoseini · 2024

What
Treats inference-time techniques as modules (generator/ensemble, fuser, ranker, critic, verifier, unit-test generator and evaluator) stacked in layers, and uses Bayesian optimization to search a space of 9,576 configurations for the best stack under a compute budget.
Result
Searched stacks beat o1, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet by 15.1% on average across instruction-following, reasoning and coding benchmarks; open-source-only stacks were 11.2% above prior open-source approaches. Bayesian optimization found the best stack in 96.0% of searches with 88.5% fewer evaluations than greedy search. Unit tests plus more samples raised CodeContests Pass@1 from 17.9% to 29.3%. The fuser helped on every benchmark (8.9% on average); verifiers helped most on reasoning tasks.
Takeaway
Don't hand-pick one trick. Build sampling, ranking, fusing and checking as swappable steps, then let a small eval set choose the stack per task.

Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters

Snell, Lee, Xu, Kumar · 2024

What
On MATH with PaLM 2-S*, compared two ways to spend test-time compute: searching against a process reward model (best-of-N, beam search, lookahead) and a fine-tuned model that revises its own answer (sequential vs parallel). Questions were binned into 5 difficulty levels by the base model's own success rate over 2048 samples.
Result
The best method depends on difficulty: sequential revision did best on easy questions, a mix of sequential and parallel on harder ones; beam search beat best-of-N on medium-hard questions but over-optimized the verifier on easy ones. Choosing per difficulty matched or beat best-of-N with up to 4x less compute (abstract: more than 4x). FLOPs-matched, a small model with test-time compute beat a 14x larger model on questions where it had some success, but pretraining was better on the hardest questions or with high inference load.
Takeaway
Estimate difficulty first (for example, by how much a few samples agree), then route: easy questions get one try plus a revision, hard ones get more parallel tries, and the hardest may need a bigger model.

How Do Large Language Monkeys Get Their Power (Laws)?

Schaeffer, Kazdan, Hughes, Juravsky, Price, Lynch, Jones, Kirk, Mirhoseini, Koyejo · 2025

What
Explains why aggregate success with k attempts follows a power law even though each single problem's failure rate falls exponentially in k: the power law comes from a heavy left tail of very hard problems in the distribution of single-attempt success rates. Tested on Pythia models on MATH and frontier models on HarmBench jailbreaking.
Result
Proved the tail near zero (density like p^(b-1)) is necessary and sufficient for a k^(-b) power law. Cases without the heavy tail (e.g. Llama 3 8B IT, jailbroken on every prompt within budget) did not show a power law. A distributional estimator of the exponent had about an order of magnitude lower relative error than curve fitting, which the authors equate to roughly 2-4 orders of magnitude less inference compute.
Takeaway
Before buying a big sampling budget, sample a little, look at the per-problem success rates, and forecast. A task with many near-zero problems will be expensive to brute-force.

from collections import Counter
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from lal.llm import client
from lal.tasks import math_problems, extract_int
from lal.checks import pass_at_k, check

SOLVE = ("Solve this problem step by step. Keep it short.\n"
         "On the last line write exactly: ANSWER: <whole number>\n\nProblem: {q}")

def gen(prompt, temperature=0.8, max_tokens=400, model_name=None):
    """One model call. Returns (text, completion tokens). Never raises, so one bad call can't stop a loop."""
    try:
        r = client.chat.completions.create(model=model_name or MODEL, messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
                                           temperature=temperature, max_tokens=max_tokens)
        text = r.choices[0].message.content or ""
        toks = getattr(r.usage, "completion_tokens", None) or max(1, len(text) // 4)
    except Exception as e:
        print("call failed:", e)
        text, toks = "", 0
    return text, toks

def majority(answers):
    """Most common answer, ignoring replies we could not parse. None if there are none."""
    votes = Counter(a for a in answers if a is not None)
    return votes.most_common(1)[0][0] if votes else None

6. Why the curve looks like a power law (no model calls)

Schaeffer et al. 2025 (How Do Large Language Monkeys Get Their Power (Laws)?) explain the shape. - For one problem with single-try success p, the chance that all k tries fail is (1 - p)^k. That falls exponentially in k. - Averaged over many problems, the failure curve looks like a power law in k (a straight line on a log-log plot). That happens when a few problems are very hard (p close to 0). This is called a heavy left tail.

We check this with exact math. Say single-try success rates follow a Beta(alpha, beta) distribution, which has a tail near 0 shaped like p^(alpha-1). Then the theory says -log(coverage) should fall like k^(-alpha).

a01_test_time_compute.ipynb · cell 8
ALPHA, BETA = 0.3, 3.0

def agg_fail(k, a=ALPHA, b=BETA):
    """Mean over problems of (1-p)^k when p ~ Beta(a, b). Exact: B(a, b+k) / B(a, b)."""
    lb = lambda x, y: math.lgamma(x) + math.lgamma(y) - math.lgamma(x + y)
    return math.exp(lb(a, b + k) - lb(a, b))

kk = np.unique(np.logspace(0, 4, 40).astype(int))
neglog_cov = np.array([-math.log(1 - agg_fail(k)) for k in kk])
big = kk >= 100
slope = np.polyfit(np.log(kk[big]), np.log(neglog_cov[big]), 1)[0]
print(f"fitted power-law exponent for k >= 100: {slope:.3f}   (theory: -alpha = {-ALPHA})")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.2))
for p in (0.3, 0.05, 0.01):
    fail = [(1 - p) ** k for k in kk]
    ax[0].plot(kk, [-math.log1p(-f) if 0 < f < 1 else np.nan for f in fail], label=f"one problem, p={p}")
ax[0].set_xscale("log"); ax[0].set_yscale("log"); ax[0].set_ylim(1e-6, 10)
ax[0].set_title("Each problem: falls off a cliff (exponential)"); ax[0].set_xlabel("k"); ax[0].set_ylabel("-log(pass@k)"); ax[0].legend(fontsize=7)
ax[1].plot(kk, neglog_cov, marker=".", label=f"many problems, p ~ Beta({ALPHA}, {BETA})")
ax[1].set_xscale("log"); ax[1].set_yscale("log")
ax[1].set_title("Average: straight line (power law)"); ax[1].set_xlabel("k"); ax[1].legend(fontsize=7)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Why a builder cares: the exponent tells you how fast more samples pay off, so you can forecast before you spend. Schaeffer et al. estimate it from the distribution of single-try success rates and report about an order of magnitude lower error than fitting the curve directly, which they describe as roughly 2-4 orders of magnitude less inference compute.

7. An Archon-style pipeline: generate, rank, fuse, verify

Saad-Falcon et al. 2024 (Archon) treat inference-time tricks as building blocks and search for the best stack. The blocks include a Generator (makes candidates), a Ranker (picks the best ones), a Fuser (merges candidates into one better answer), and a Verifier (keeps only answers whose reasoning checks out).

We build one fixed stack by hand on the 4 hard problems: reuse the 6 samples, then rank (1 call), fuse (1 call), verify the fused answer (1 call). If the verifier says NO, we fall back to majority vote. That is 12 calls.

a01_test_time_compute.ipynb · cell 9
def numbered(cands, limit=600):
    return "\n\n".join(f"Candidate {i + 1}:\n{c['text'][:limit]}" for i, c in enumerate(cands))

RANK = ("Problem: {q}\n\n{c}\n\nWhich candidate has the correct final answer? Check the arithmetic. "
        "Reply with the candidate number only.")
FUSE = ("Problem: {q}\n\nHere are several attempts. Some may be wrong.\n\n{c}\n\n"
        "Use the attempts to write one careful, correct solution. On the last line write exactly: ANSWER: <whole number>")
VERIFY = ("Problem: {q}\n\nProposed solution:\n{s}\n\nCheck every step and the arithmetic. "
          "Is the final answer correct? Reply YES or NO only.")

def archon(p):
    cands = SAMPLES[p["id"]]
    r, _ = gen(RANK.format(q=p["question"], c=numbered(cands)), temperature=0, max_tokens=10)
    digits = [int(x) for x in "".join(ch if ch.isdigit() else " " for ch in r).split()]
    idx = digits[0] - 1 if digits and 1 <= digits[0] <= len(cands) else 0
    fused, _ = gen(FUSE.format(q=p["question"], c=numbered(cands)), temperature=0, max_tokens=500)
    v, _ = gen(VERIFY.format(q=p["question"], s=fused), temperature=0, max_tokens=5)
    fused_ans = extract_int(fused)
    final = fused_ans if "YES" in v.upper() else majority([c["answer"] for c in cands])
    return {"first": cands[0]["answer"], "majority": majority([c["answer"] for c in cands]),
            "ranked": cands[idx]["answer"], "fused": fused_ans, "fused+verify": final,
            "oracle": p["answer"] if any(c["ok"] for c in cands) else None}

with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
    ARCH = dict(zip([p["id"] for p in HARD], ex.map(archon, HARD)))
for method in ["first", "majority", "ranked", "fused", "fused+verify", "oracle"]:
    right = sum(ARCH[p["id"]][method] == p["answer"] for p in HARD)
    print(f"{method:13} {right}/{len(HARD)}")

With 4 problems this is noisy, so don't read too much into one run. The paper's lesson is about combining blocks: Archon reports that across its benchmarks the Fuser helped on every one (8.9% on average), the Ranker came within 2.7% of oracle selection on MT-Bench and Arena-Hard-Auto, and the Verifier helped most on reasoning tasks. Their searched stacks beat o1, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet by 15.1% on average.

8. Spend compute by difficulty (Snell et al. 2024)

Snell et al. 2024 (Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally) ask: with a fixed budget, should you sample in parallel (many independent tries, then vote) or sequentially (one try, then revise it, then revise again)? Their answer: it depends on difficulty. On easy questions, sequential revision worked best. On harder ones, a mix of both did. Picking the strategy per question beat best-of-N with up to 4x less compute.

First, how hard is each problem for this model? Snell et al. measure difficulty as the model's own success rate over many samples, not a human label. We do the same with our 6 samples.

Now a budget of 3 generations per problem, two ways: - parallel: majority vote over 3 of our existing samples (0 new calls); - sequential: take sample 1, then ask the model twice to check and fix its own work (2 new calls per problem, 16 in total).

Note: Snell et al. fine-tuned a special revision model. We only prompt, so our revisions are weaker than theirs.

a01_test_time_compute.ipynb · cell 11
REVISE = ("Problem: {q}\n\nYour previous attempt:\n{prev}\n\nCheck each step of the attempt. If you find a mistake, fix it. "
          "Write the full corrected solution. On the last line write exactly: ANSWER: <whole number>")

def sequential(p, rounds=2):
    text = SAMPLES[p["id"]][0]["text"]
    for _ in range(rounds):
        new, _ = gen(REVISE.format(q=p["question"], prev=text[:1500]), temperature=0.3)
        text = new or text
    return extract_int(text)

with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
    SEQ = dict(zip([p["id"] for p in PROBS], ex.map(sequential, PROBS)))
PAR = {p["id"]: majority([s["answer"] for s in SAMPLES[p["id"]][:3]]) for p in PROBS}

for name, ps in [("easy", EASY), ("hard", HARD)]:
    par = sum(PAR[p["id"]] == p["answer"] for p in ps)
    seq = sum(SEQ[p["id"]] == p["answer"] for p in ps)
    print(f"{name}: parallel (vote of 3) {par}/{len(ps)}   sequential (1 + 2 revisions) {seq}/{len(ps)}")

How to read this: a "compute-optimal" policy picks, for each difficulty level, whichever strategy wins there. To do that in production you need a cheap difficulty predictor (Snell et al. used a verifier's scores; you could use agreement between a few samples). Snell et al. also found the trade has limits: on the hardest questions, or when you serve a lot of traffic, a bigger pretrained model was the better use of compute.

Traps
  • Reporting coverage (pass@k) as if it were accuracy. Coverage assumes you can always spot the right try; in production you usually can't.
  • Adding samples without a verifier. Brown et al. saw majority vote and reward models stall near 100 samples while coverage kept rising.
  • Sampling at temperature 0. Every try is the same, so more tries add nothing.
  • Counting calls instead of tokens. A fuser or revision call reads all candidates, so its cost grows with the number of candidates.
  • Assuming test-time compute always replaces a bigger model. Snell et al. found pretraining wins on the hardest questions and at high inference volume.
  • Choosing the strategy per difficulty on the same small set you then report on. That overfits; hold out data.

Check yourself

Coverage at k=100 is 90% but majority vote at k=100 is 45%. What is missing?

A good verifier. The model can produce the right answer, but the picker can't find it: that 45-point difference is the generation-verification gap.

Why can a per-problem failure rate fall exponentially while the average follows a power law?

Because the average mixes problems of different difficulty. A heavy tail of problems with near-zero single-try success makes the average fall slowly, like a power law (Schaeffer et al. 2025).

For an easy question, should you spend 4 calls on 4 parallel samples or on 1 sample plus 3 revisions?

Snell et al. found sequential revision best on easy questions, and a mix of both on harder ones. Test on your own task, since they used a fine-tuned revision model.
