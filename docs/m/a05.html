Part 2 · A05

Train-time scaling and RL

Open in Colab
12 min readPlain-English lesson, then the deeper material

How a model can get better by practicing on problems a program can grade. You'll understand STaR, GRPO and DAPO, and run a tiny training loop.

The big idea

Until now we made models smarter at answer time, by letting them think longer. This lesson spends the effort earlier, at training time. Training means nudging the model's internal numbers (its weights) so it behaves differently from now on.

Think of a junior associate doing practice problems with an answer key. They try a problem. They check the key. If they got it right, they remember how. If they got it wrong, they try to work out why. Nobody has to write them a full lesson. The answer key does the teaching.

That answer key is the heart of this lesson. If a program can check the answer, the model can learn from its own attempts. Keep or reward what the checker accepts. Push away from what it rejects.

Why it matters for us

  • A firm running its own model cannot afford to hire people to write thousands of worked examples. This method lets the model write its own examples, and a cheap checker filters them.
  • Many contract checks have a clear right answer. "Is the liability cap at most 12 months of fees?" is yes or no. A program can grade that, so it can drive training.
  • The whole loop fits on a free GPU (graphics card) with a small open model. You don't need a cloud lab to try it.

How it works, step by step

  1. Find a task with a checkable answer. We need a verifiable reward (a score computed by a program, like an exact-match test, not by a human). Arithmetic works. So does "did the model flag the right clause?"

  2. STaR: let the model write its own study notes (the STaR paper, 2022). The model reasons through many problems step by step. We keep its reasoning only when the final answer is right. For the ones it got wrong, we show it the correct answer as a hint and ask it to explain. This is called rationalization. If the explanation now lands on the right answer, we keep it, but we store it without the hint. Then we fine-tune (train further on these examples) and repeat. In the paper, a 6-billion-parameter model reached 72.5% on a common-sense quiz this way, close to a fine-tuned model 30 times larger at 73.0%.

  3. Reinforcement learning, from zero. Reinforcement learning (RL) is learning by trial and reward, like training a dog with treats. The model tries something. It gets a score. Actions that scored well become more likely next time. The key quantity is the advantage: how much better one attempt did than expected. Positive means "do more of this". Negative means "do less".

  4. GRPO: grade on a curve (the DeepSeekMath paper, 2024). Older RL methods train a second network, a "critic", just to guess what score to expect. That costs a lot of memory. GRPO skips it. For each question it samples a group of answers (the paper used 64). It then compares each answer to the group average. Above average gets pushed up. Below average gets pushed down. It is like grading a class on a curve instead of hiring a second teacher to predict scores. In the paper, this step lifted a 7B math model from 46.8% to 51.7% on a hard math test. One honest finding: RL made the model more reliable at answers it could already sometimes find. It did not clearly teach brand-new skills.

  5. DAPO: four repairs to GRPO (the DAPO paper, 2025). Plain GRPO wastes effort on long reasoning. DAPO adds four fixes:

  6. Clip-higher: let rare good word choices grow faster, so the model keeps exploring.
  7. Dynamic sampling: if every answer in a group got the same score, throw that group out and sample a new question.
  8. Token-level loss: count every word equally, so long bad answers don't get off lightly.
  9. Overlong shaping: a gentle penalty as answers near the length limit, instead of a harsh zero for being cut off.

On a hard math contest, plain GRPO scored 30 and full DAPO scored 50.

Worked example

Say the firm's model must answer: "Does this clause cap liability at 12 months of fees or less? End with Answer: yes or no." A program reads the last line and compares it to the right answer.

We ask the model the same question four times. Three answers say "yes" correctly. One says "no". The rewards are [1, 1, 1, 0]. The average is 0.75. The three right answers sit above average, so they get a positive advantage. The wrong one gets a negative advantage. The model shifts toward the reasoning that worked.

Now suppose all four were right: [1, 1, 1, 1]. Every answer equals the average. Every advantage is zero. That question taught nothing, even though we paid to generate four answers. This is what DAPO's dynamic sampling fixes.

The notebook computes the advantage in two lines:

def grpo_advantages(r, eps=1e-4):
    r = np.asarray(r, dtype=float)
    return (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + eps)
  • The first line names the function. r is the list of rewards for one group. eps is a tiny number to avoid dividing by zero.
  • The second line turns the list into an array of decimals.
  • The third line subtracts the group average from each reward, then divides by how spread out the rewards are. That is "grading on a curve".

Common mistakes

  • Problems that are too easy or too hard. Every group is all-right or all-wrong, so nothing is learned. Pick problems at the edge of what the model can do.
  • A reward the model can game. If the checker takes "the last number in the text", the model may learn to print lots of numbers. Check the format strictly.
  • Right answer, wrong reasoning. STaR and score-based rewards both accept lucky guesses. Spot-check the reasoning, especially rationalized examples.
  • Trusting the training score. The reward going up during training is not proof. Always test on held-out problems (ones the model never trained on), before and after.

Check your understanding

A group of four answers gets rewards [1, 1, 1, 1]. What are the GRPO advantages, and what does DAPO do?

All zero, because every reward equals the average. The group teaches nothing, so DAPO's dynamic sampling drops it and samples another question.

In STaR, why is the hint removed before training on a rationalized example?

At test time the model won't be told the answer. It must learn to produce that reasoning from the question alone.

What does GRPO use instead of a separate critic network?

The average (and spread) of the scores of the other answers to the same question.

Key terms, papers, evidence, the full notebook code and worked solutions.

CS329A Lecture 6 (Oct 10)

If a program can check the answer, the model can improve by training on its own attempts: keep or reward what the checker accepts, and push away from what it rejects. STaR does this with supervised fine-tuning; GRPO and DAPO do it with reinforcement learning.

Key terms

Rationalization
In STaR, asking the model to explain a problem it got wrong while showing it the correct answer as a hint, then training on that explanation without the hint.Source: Zelikman et al. 2022
Verifiable reward
A reward computed by a program (exact answer match, unit tests), not by a human or a learned judge. It is what makes RL on reasoning cheap and hard to fool.Source: lecture
GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)
Sample a group of answers per prompt; each answer's advantage is its reward minus the group mean, divided by the group std. No critic model needed.Source: Shao et al. 2024
Advantage
How much better one answer did than expected. Positive means 'do more of this', negative means 'do less'.Source: RL basics
Clip-higher
Use a larger upper clip bound than lower bound on the probability ratio (0.28 vs 0.2), so rare good tokens can grow and the model keeps exploring.Source: Yu et al. 2025
Dynamic sampling
Drop prompt groups where every answer got the same reward (zero advantage, zero gradient) and sample new prompts to fill the batch.Source: Yu et al. 2025
LoRA
Train small low-rank add-on matrices instead of all model weights. Cuts memory so RL fits a free GPU.Source: Hu et al. 2021

The papers

STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning

Zelikman, Wu, Mu & Goodman · 2022

What
A loop on GPT-J (6B): generate step-by-step rationales with a few examples, keep the ones that reach the right answer, and for wrong ones generate a rationale again with the correct answer given as a hint ('rationalization'), stored without the hint. Fine-tune the original model on everything kept, then repeat.
Result
CommonsenseQA dev accuracy 72.5% with rationalization (68.8% without), vs 60.0% for GPT-J fine-tuned to answer directly and 73.0% for a fine-tuned GPT-3 that is 30x larger. GSM8K: 10.7% vs 5.8% for direct fine-tuning.
Takeaway
Before buying labeled reasoning data, let your model write it and filter by a checker. Add the answer-as-hint step to cover the problems it cannot yet solve, and watch for right answers with wrong reasoning.

DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models

Shao et al. · 2024

What
Continued pre-training of a 7B model on 120B math tokens, then introduces GRPO: a PPO variant with no critic (value) model. For each question it samples a group of answers (64 in the paper) and uses each answer's reward relative to the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation, as its advantage, with a KL penalty (0.04) added directly to the loss.
Result
51.7% on MATH without tools or voting (60.9% with self-consistency over 64 samples). The GRPO stage alone took DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K and 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH. RL raised Maj@K but not Pass@K.
Takeaway
GRPO is the cheapest RL recipe to start with: no critic network, only a reward function and a group of samples per prompt. Expect it to make good answers more reliable more than to teach brand-new skills (the Maj@K vs Pass@K finding).

DAPO: An Open-Source LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale

Yu et al. · 2025

What
Four fixes to GRPO for long reasoning: Clip-Higher (clip ratio at 0.2 below but 0.28 above, to avoid entropy collapse), Dynamic Sampling (drop prompts where all answers are right or all wrong), a token-level loss (average over all tokens, not per answer), and Overlong Reward Shaping (filter or softly penalize answers that hit the length limit). It also removes the KL penalty.
Result
50 points on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-32B base, vs 47 for DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B, using 50% of the training steps. Ablation: naive GRPO 30, +overlong filtering 36, +clip-higher 38, +soft overlong punishment 41, +token-level loss 42, +dynamic sampling 50.
Takeaway
When you run GRPO, turn on these fixes: in TRL, loss_type='dapo' (now the default), epsilon_high=0.28, a length penalty or mask_truncated_completions=True, and keep training prompts at the edge of what the model can solve so groups are not all-equal.

1. A task with exact answers

RL from rewards only works when a program can grade the answer. We generate arithmetic problems with a fixed seed, so every run sees the same problems and every answer is either right or wrong. The model must end with a line Answer: <number>. We read that line with a regex. If it is missing we fall back to the last number in the text.

a05_rl_training.ipynb · cell 3
def make_problems(n, seed, hard=False):
    """Seeded arithmetic problems. hard=True gives 3-step problems that trip up small models."""
    rng, out = random.Random(seed), []
    for _ in range(n):
        if hard:
            a, b, c, d = rng.randint(12, 49), rng.randint(3, 9), rng.randint(10, 99), rng.randint(10, 99)
            q, ans = f"What is {a} * {b} - {c} + {d}?", a * b - c + d
        else:
            op = rng.choice(["+", "-", "*"])
            if op == "*":
                a, b = rng.randint(3, 9), rng.randint(11, 39)
            else:
                a, b = rng.randint(10, 99), rng.randint(10, 99)
            q, ans = f"What is {a} {op} {b}?", eval(f"{a}{op}{b}")       # safe: we built the string ourselves
        out.append({"question": q, "answer": int(ans)})
    return out

def extract_int(text):
    """Number after 'Answer:' if present, otherwise the last integer in the text. None if no number."""
    text = text or ""
    m = re.findall(r"Answer:\s*\**\s*(-?\d[\d,]*)", text, re.I)
    if not m:
        m = re.findall(r"-?\d[\d,]*", text)
    if not m:
        return None
    try:
        return int(m[-1].replace(",", ""))
    except ValueError:
        return None

def has_answer_line(text):
    return bool(re.search(r"Answer:\s*\**\s*-?\d", text or "", re.I))

INSTR = "Think step by step in one or two short lines, then finish with the line 'Answer: <number>'."
print(make_problems(3, seed=0), extract_int("so 12*3=36. Answer: 36"), extract_int("I think 41"))

2. STaR: the model writes its own training data

STaR (Zelikman et al. 2022, STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning) is a simple loop: 1. Ask the model to reason and answer many problems. 2. Keep the reasoning only when the final answer is right. 3. For the problems it got wrong, show it the correct answer as a hint and ask it to explain. This is called rationalization. If the explanation now ends at the right answer, keep it, but store it without the hint, as if the model had found it alone. 4. Fine-tune on the kept examples and repeat.

In the paper, GPT-J (6B) with STaR reached 72.5% on CommonsenseQA, close to 73.0% for a fine-tuned GPT-3 that is 30 times larger. Here we do steps 1-3 with the 3B Ollama model and save the dataset. Step 4 is the same supervised fine-tuning you would do with any chat dataset.

a05_rl_training.ipynb · cell 4
star_problems = make_problems(16, seed=1, hard=True)

def rationale(p, hint=None):
    prompt = f"{p['question']}\n{INSTR}"
    if hint is not None:
        prompt += f"\n(Hint: the correct answer is {hint}. Explain the steps that lead to it.)"
    try:
        return ask(prompt, model_name=STUDENT, temperature=0.7, max_tokens=200)
    except Exception as e:
        return f"(error: {e})"

t0 = time.time()
with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
    first = list(ex.map(rationale, star_problems))                       # 16 calls
wrong = [i for i, (p, r) in enumerate(zip(star_problems, first)) if extract_int(r) != p["answer"]]
with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
    hinted = dict(zip(wrong, ex.map(lambda i: rationale(star_problems[i], hint=star_problems[i]["answer"]), wrong)))  # <= 16 calls

sft = []
for i, (p, r) in enumerate(zip(star_problems, first)):
    if i not in wrong:
        sft.append({"source": "self", "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": f"{p['question']}\n{INSTR}"},
                                                   {"role": "assistant", "content": r.strip()}]})
    elif extract_int(hinted[i]) == p["answer"] and "hint" not in hinted[i].lower():
        # the prompt we store has NO hint: the model learns to produce this reasoning on its own
        sft.append({"source": "rationalized", "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": f"{p['question']}\n{INSTR}"},
                                                           {"role": "assistant", "content": hinted[i].strip()}]})
with open("star_sft.jsonl", "w") as f:
    for row in sft:
        f.write(json.dumps(row) + "\n")

n_self = sum(r["source"] == "self" for r in sft)
print(f"{len(star_problems)} problems: {len(star_problems) - len(wrong)} right on the first try, {len(wrong)} wrong")
print(f"kept {n_self} self-made + {len(sft) - n_self} rationalized examples -> star_sft.jsonl  [{time.time() - t0:.0f}s]")
if sft:
    print("\nexample:", sft[0]["messages"][1]["content"][:300])

Two things to notice: - The checker is the teacher. No human wrote any of these explanations. We only kept the ones whose final number was right. - A right answer can still have a wrong explanation. Rationalized examples are the riskiest: the model knew the answer and may have bent the steps to fit. We drop any reply that mentions the hint, and in real use you would also check the steps (for arithmetic you can recompute each line).

3. GRPO by hand: grade against the group, not against a critic

GRPO (Shao et al. 2024, DeepSeekMath) is the RL method behind many recent reasoning models. For each prompt: 1. Sample a group of G answers (the paper used G = 64). 2. Score each one with the reward. 3. The advantage of an answer is how much better it did than its group: (reward - group mean) / group std. 4. Raise the probability of tokens in answers with positive advantage, lower it for negative ones.

PPO needs a second network (a "critic" or value model) to guess the expected reward. GRPO uses the group mean instead, which saves a lot of memory. In the paper, GRPO lifted DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K and from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH.

Let us get real rewards: 4 samples each for 3 problems from the 3B model (12 calls).

a05_rl_training.ipynb · cell 5
REWARD_CORRECT, REWARD_FORMAT = 1.0, 0.2

def score(text, gold):
    return (REWARD_CORRECT if extract_int(text) == gold else 0.0) + (REWARD_FORMAT if has_answer_line(text) else 0.0)

G = 4
group_problems = make_problems(3, seed=2, hard=True)
jobs = [p for p in group_problems for _ in range(G)]
with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
    outs = list(ex.map(rationale, jobs))
groups = [np.array([score(o, p["answer"]) for o, p in zip(outs[k * G:(k + 1) * G], jobs[k * G:(k + 1) * G])])
          for k in range(len(group_problems))]

def grpo_advantages(r, eps=1e-4):
    r = np.asarray(r, dtype=float)
    return (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + eps)

for p, r in zip(group_problems, groups):
    print(f"{p['question']:<32} rewards {r}  ->  advantages {grpo_advantages(r).round(2)}")

Read the output like this: a group where every answer got the same reward has advantage 0 for all of them. That prompt teaches the model nothing, even though we paid to generate it. DAPO fixes exactly this.

4. What DAPO changes (Yu et al. 2025, DAPO)

DAPO starts from GRPO and adds four fixes. In their ablation on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-32B, plain GRPO scored 30, and the full DAPO recipe scored 50. That beat DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B (47) with 50% of its training steps. DAPO also drops the KL penalty.

Fix Problem it solves In TRL's GRPOConfig
Clip-higher: clip the probability ratio at 1-0.2 below but 1+0.28 above With symmetric clipping, rare but good tokens cannot grow fast, so the model stops exploring ("entropy collapse") epsilon=0.2, epsilon_high=0.28
Dynamic sampling: drop groups where all rewards are equal, sample more until the batch is full Those groups give zero gradient and waste the batch not built in; we measure it below
Token-level loss: average the loss over all tokens in the batch, not per answer first Per-answer averaging lets a long bad answer count the same as a short one loss_type="dapo" (TRL's default now)
Overlong reward shaping: soft penalty that grows as an answer nears the length limit Cut-off answers get a reward of 0 for the wrong reason, which adds noise a reward function, below; or mask_truncated_completions=True

Here is dynamic sampling on the rewards we just collected, and the soft overlong penalty as a function.

a05_rl_training.ipynb · cell 6
def dynamic_sampling(groups):
    """Keep only groups whose rewards are not all equal (DAPO)."""
    return [g for g in groups if np.ptp(g) > 0]

def overlong_penalty(n_tokens, l_max=96, l_cache=32):
    """DAPO soft overlong punishment: 0 until l_max - l_cache tokens, then down to -1 at l_max."""
    if n_tokens <= l_max - l_cache:
        return 0.0
    if n_tokens <= l_max:
        return (l_max - l_cache - n_tokens) / l_cache
    return -1.0

kept = dynamic_sampling(groups)
print(f"dynamic sampling keeps {len(kept)} of {len(groups)} groups")
print("overlong penalty at 40, 70, 90, 96 tokens:", [round(overlong_penalty(n), 2) for n in (40, 70, 90, 96)])

5. A real GRPO run: SmolLM2-360M + LoRA on a T4

Now we train for real with Hugging Face TRL's GRPOTrainer. - Model: HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct, a small open model. We train only a LoRA adapter (low-rank extra weights, about 1% of the model), so memory stays small. - Rewards: +1.0 for the exact answer, +0.2 for the Answer: line, and DAPO's soft overlong penalty (scaled by 0.5). - Group size 8, completions of at most 96 tokens, 120 steps of 2 prompts each. - Precision: the T4 has no bfloat16, so we keep the weights in float32 and use float16 mixed precision. TRL's config turns bf16 on by default, so we switch it off explicitly.

A greedy evaluation on the held-out problems, so we can compare before and after.

a05_rl_training.ipynb · cell 8
@torch.no_grad()
def evaluate(model, rows, batch=30):
    model.eval()
    right, fmt = 0, 0
    for i in range(0, len(rows), batch):
        chunk = rows[i:i + batch]
        texts = [tok.apply_chat_template(to_prompt(r["question"]), tokenize=False, add_generation_prompt=True) for r in chunk]
        enc = tok(texts, return_tensors="pt", padding=True).to(model.device)
        out = model.generate(**enc, max_new_tokens=MAX_NEW, do_sample=False, pad_token_id=tok.pad_token_id)
        for r, ids in zip(chunk, out[:, enc["input_ids"].shape[1]:]):
            t = tok.decode(ids, skip_special_tokens=True)
            right += extract_int(t) == r["answer"]
            fmt += has_answer_line(t)
    return right / len(rows), fmt / len(rows)

acc_before, fmt_before = evaluate(policy, test_rows)
print(f"BEFORE training: accuracy {acc_before:.0%}, uses 'Answer:' line {fmt_before:.0%}")

The reward functions. TRL calls each one with the batch of completions and every extra dataset column (here answer and question) as keyword arguments, and expects one float per completion. Our prompts are chat messages, so each completion arrives as a list of messages. The helper handles both shapes. We also log every reward with its question so we can study the groups afterwards.

a05_rl_training.ipynb · cell 9
reward_log = []          # (question, total reward) for every completion TRL generates

def _text(c):
    if isinstance(c, list):                                   # conversational format
        return " ".join(m.get("content", "") for m in c if isinstance(m, dict))
    return c or ""

def correct_reward(completions, answer, question=None, **kwargs):
    texts = [_text(c) for c in completions]
    rs = [REWARD_CORRECT if extract_int(t) == int(a) else 0.0 for t, a in zip(texts, answer)]
    fm = [REWARD_FORMAT if has_answer_line(t) else 0.0 for t in texts]
    for q, r, f in zip(question or [""] * len(rs), rs, fm):
        reward_log.append((q, r + f))
    return rs

def format_reward(completions, **kwargs):
    return [REWARD_FORMAT if has_answer_line(_text(c)) else 0.0 for c in completions]

def overlong_reward(completion_ids, **kwargs):
    return [0.5 * overlong_penalty(len(ids), l_max=MAX_NEW, l_cache=32) for ids in completion_ids]

Traps
  • Training on problems that are too easy or too hard: every group is all-correct or all-wrong, the advantages are all zero, and nothing is learned even though the run looks busy.
  • A reward that can be gamed. If the parser takes 'the last number in the text', the model may learn to print many numbers. Check the format strictly or reward it separately.
  • Right answer, wrong reasoning. STaR and outcome rewards both accept lucky or bent reasoning; spot-check rationales, especially rationalized ones.
  • bf16 on a T4. The T4 has no bfloat16 and TRL's GRPOConfig turns bf16 on by default; set bf16=False and use fp16 mixed precision or fp32.
  • Reading the training reward as the result. Always compare accuracy on a held-out set before and after.

Check yourself

A group of 4 answers gets rewards [1, 1, 1, 1]. What are the GRPO advantages, and what does DAPO do with this group?

All zero (every reward equals the mean), so the group gives no gradient. DAPO's dynamic sampling drops it and samples another prompt.

In STaR, why is the hint removed from the prompt before fine-tuning on a rationalized example?

So the model learns to produce that reasoning from the question alone, as it must at test time, not to depend on being told the answer.

What does GRPO use instead of PPO's critic (value) model?

The mean (and std) of the rewards of the other answers sampled for the same prompt.
