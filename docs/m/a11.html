Guest lectures and reasoning frontiers

Open in Colab
12 min readPlain-English lesson, then the deeper material

The reasoning tricks behind today's models, tried on a small local one. You'll understand chain of thought, self-consistency, least-to-most, CoT-decoding, and where the guest talks…

The big idea

The guest talks in this course trace one arc. Models learned to reason step by step. Then to sample many answers and vote. Then to learn from rewards a program can check. Now they act in software and in the physical world.

The heart of it is simple. Think of a junior associate asked a tricky question. If you demand a one-word answer on the spot, they guess. If you let them write out their steps, they do better. If you ask five associates separately and they mostly agree, you can trust the answer more. If they disagree, you send it to a partner.

One note: the course assigned no papers for these talks. Every reading on this page is our pick of the speaker's best-known public work, not the course's list.

Why it matters for us

  • These tricks need no training. They work on any local model today, with just prompts and a few extra calls.
  • Voting gives you a free confidence score. That tells the firm which contract answers can go straight through and which need a lawyer's eyes.
  • The big math wins came from rewards a program could check exactly. The firm's labeled contracts are that kind of check, at small scale.

How it works, step by step

  1. Chain of thought: show your work (the Chain-of-Thought paper, 2022). Chain of thought (CoT) means the model writes its reasoning steps before the final answer. With eight worked examples, a very large model reached the best score at the time on grade-school math problems. Today's chat models are trained to do this, so "think step by step" is usually enough. The gain grows with model size. Tiny models can write steps that look right but aren't.

  2. Self-consistency: ask several times, take the majority (the Self-Consistency paper, 2022). Sample several reasoning paths with some randomness (a temperature above 0, a setting that makes answers vary). Take the most common final answer. Why does it work? There are many paths to the right answer, but wrong answers tend to scatter. The paper reports a gain of 17.9% over single-chain CoT on grade-school math. The share of samples that agree is the agreement rate, a cheap confidence score.

  3. Least-to-most: split the problem first (the Least-to-Most paper, 2022). First ask the model to break the problem into easier sub-questions. Then solve them in order, feeding each answer into the next. This helps most when the real problem is harder than the examples. On one test of following longer instructions, it reached at least 99%, against 16% for plain CoT.

  4. CoT-decoding: reasoning without a prompt (the CoT-decoding paper, 2024). Normally a model uses greedy decoding: it always picks the single most likely next word. CoT-decoding branches instead. At the first word of the answer, it tries the top 10 alternatives and continues each one greedily. Some branches jump to a number. Others start "First..." and reason their way there. The branch whose answer the model is most confident about wins. On one 7B base model, math accuracy went from 9.9% with greedy decoding to 25.1%. The reasoning was in the model all along. Greedy decoding just hid it.

  5. Checkable rewards beat clever prompts (Thang Luong's talk). AlphaGeometry (2024) paired a language model that suggests extra lines or points with an exact logic engine that does the proof. It solved 25 of 30 olympiad geometry problems. AlphaProof (2024) used reinforcement learning (learning by trial and reward) in Lean, a language whose proofs a computer can check exactly. It scored 28 of 42 at the 2024 math olympiad, but some problems took up to three days. In 2025, Gemini Deep Think scored 35 of 42 in plain language within the 4.5-hour limit, using "parallel thinking" (exploring several solutions at once).

  6. The other talks, in one line each. Post-training (everything after the first big training run, like instruction tuning and RL) turns a text predictor into an agent (Melvin Johnson). An agent can improve from its own past attempts, if it can see them (Misha Laskin's Algorithm Distillation paper, 2022). The same recipe works for robots: start from a big pretrained model, add images and actions, then fine-tune (Danny Driess's PaLM-E and π0 papers).

Worked example

The firm asks its local model: "This contract's monthly fee is X and the liability cap is Y. Is the cap within 12 months of fees?" It is a two-step problem: multiply, then compare.

Direct answer. "Answer yes or no only." The model has no room to work it out. It guesses.

Chain of thought. "Think step by step." It computes 12 times the fee, compares it to the cap, then answers.

Self-consistency. Ask the same question five times. The notebook does exactly this on math problems:

K = 5
votes = [Counter(a for a in s if a is not None).most_common(1) for s in samples]
sc = [v[0][0] if v else None for v in votes]
agree = [v[0][1] / K if v else 0 for v in votes]
  • Line 1 sets the number of samples per question to 5.
  • Line 2 counts the answers for each question, skipping blanks, and finds the most common one.
  • Line 3 takes that most common answer as the final vote.
  • Line 4 computes agreement: how many of the 5 samples matched the vote.

The business use. If 5 of 5 agree, let it through. If only 2 of 5 agree, send it to a lawyer. You trade some review work for much higher accuracy on what passes unreviewed. But beware: agreement measures consistency, not truth. A model can be wrong the same way every time.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing a short answer on a multi-step problem. "Only the number" removes the reasoning the model needs.
  • Reading high agreement as truth. Voting can't fix a mistake the model makes every time.
  • Expecting big CoT gains from tiny models. The gain grows with size. Check a sample of the steps.
  • Copying the olympiad recipe without a checker. RL worked there because answers could be verified exactly.

Check your understanding

How is self-consistency different from CoT-decoding?

Self-consistency samples whole reasoning paths from a step-by-step prompt and votes. CoT-decoding uses no prompt, branches only on the first word, continues greedily, and picks the most confident answer.

Why does least-to-most help on problems harder than the examples?

It breaks the problem into easier pieces the model can handle. Earlier answers feed later ones, so the model never has to solve the whole thing at once.

What made reinforcement learning work for AlphaProof?

Lean, a proof language a computer can check exactly. That gave a reliable reward.

Key terms

The model writes its reasoning steps before the final answer.Source: Wei et al. 2022
Self-consistency

Agreement rate

Least-to-most prompting
Decompose into easier sub-questions, then solve them in order using earlier answers.Source: Zhou et al. 2022
Greedy decoding
Always picking the single most likely next token.Source: general
CoT-decoding

Answer confidence (Δ)

Post-training

The papers

Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, Zhou · 2022

What
Prompting with a few worked examples that show intermediate reasoning steps, tested on three large models across arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning.
Result
A 540B model with eight chain-of-thought examples reached state-of-the-art accuracy on GSM8K, beating a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier.
Takeaway
Let the model show its work before the answer on multi-step tasks; then grade only the final answer with code.

Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Wang, Wei, Schuurmans, Le, Chi, Narang, Chowdhery, Zhou · 2022

What
Sample several diverse reasoning paths instead of one greedy path and take the most consistent final answer.
Result
Over chain-of-thought: GSM8K +17.9%, SVAMP +11.0%, AQuA +12.2%, StrategyQA +6.4%, ARC-challenge +3.9%.
Takeaway
Majority voting is a cheap accuracy boost, and the agreement rate is a free confidence score you can use to route low-agreement answers to a person.

Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Zhou, Schärli, Hou, Wei, Scales, Wang, Schuurmans, Cui, Bousquet, Le, Chi · 2022

What
Two stages: decompose a problem into simpler sub-problems, then solve them in order, feeding each answer into the next. Aimed at problems harder than the prompt examples.
Result
On SCAN with code-davinci-002 and 14 examples: at least 99% accuracy on every split, including the length split, vs 16% for chain-of-thought.
Takeaway
When tasks are longer than your examples, have the agent write a plan of sub-tasks first and execute them one by one.

Chain-of-Thought Reasoning Without Prompting (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Wang, Zhou · 2024

What
Instead of greedy decoding, branch on the top-k alternatives for the first token (k = 10 by default), continue each greedily, and rank paths by answer confidence: the average gap between top-1 and top-2 token probabilities over the answer tokens.
Result
Mistral-7B (pre-trained) on GSM8K: greedy 9.9%, self-consistency without a CoT prompt 12.9%, CoT-decoding 25.1%. PaLM-2 Large: 34.8% greedy to 63.2%. Instruction-tuned Mistral-7B also improved (31.2% to 38.2%).
Takeaway
Reasoning paths often exist inside a model even when greedy decoding misses them; answer-token confidence is a useful signal for picking among candidates.

Solving olympiad geometry without human demonstrations (AlphaGeometry; our pick for Thang Luong's talk)

Trinh, Wu, Le, He, Luong · 2024

What
A neural language model proposes auxiliary constructions and a symbolic deduction engine does rigorous proofs, trained on 100 million synthetic theorems and proofs with no human demonstrations.
Result
Solved 25 of 30 recent olympiad geometry problems (IMO-AG-30); the previous best method solved 10, and an average human gold medalist about 25.9.
Takeaway
Pair a creative model with an exact checker, and generate your own training data when human data is scarce.

AI achieves silver-medal standard solving International Mathematical Olympiad problems (AlphaProof + AlphaGeometry 2 blog; our pick)

Google DeepMind · 2024

What
AlphaProof couples a pre-trained language model with AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning in the Lean proof language, trained on about a million problems auto-formalized by Gemini. AlphaGeometry 2 handled geometry.
Result
28 of 42 points at IMO 2024 (4 of 6 problems), silver level; some problems took up to three days. AlphaGeometry 2 solved 83% of IMO geometry problems from the past 25 years (predecessor 53%).
Takeaway
Reinforcement learning works best when a machine can check the answer exactly; formal verifiers are such checkers.

Advanced version of Gemini with Deep Think officially achieves gold-medal standard at the IMO (blog; our pick)

Google DeepMind · 2025

What
A Gemini model reasoning end to end in natural language, with 'parallel thinking' over several candidate solutions and new reinforcement-learning techniques for multi-step reasoning and proofs.
Result
35 of 42 points at IMO 2025 (5 of 6 problems), gold level, within the 4.5-hour limit, graded by IMO coordinators.
Takeaway
Parallel exploration plus RL on hard, checkable problems moved a general model past a specialized formal system in one year.

Google's Multilingual Neural Machine Translation System: Enabling Zero-Shot Translation (our pick for Melvin Johnson's talk)

Johnson, Schuster, Le, Krikun, Wu, Chen, Thorat, Viégas, Wattenberg, Corrado, Hughes, Dean · 2017

What
One translation model for many languages, with only a target-language token added to the input and no extra parameters. Background for the speaker, who now leads Gemini post-training (per his site); for his post-training work see the Gemini report (arXiv 2312.11805), where he is an author.
Result
Matched state-of-the-art English to French and beat it for English to German on WMT'14; translated between language pairs never seen together in training (zero-shot).
Takeaway
A single general model with the right training signal can replace many special-purpose ones; post-training is where that signal is added.

In-context Reinforcement Learning with Algorithm Distillation (our pick for Misha Laskin's talk)

Laskin, Wang, Oh, Parisotto, Spencer, Steigerwald, Strouse, Hansen, Filos, Brooks, Gazeau, Sahni, Singh, Mnih · 2022

What
Train a causal transformer on the learning histories of an RL algorithm, so it learns to improve its policy in context, with no weight updates. Laskin later co-founded Reflection AI (March 2025), which focuses on autonomous coding agents.
Result
Improved its policy entirely in context in sparse-reward, combinatorial and pixel-based environments, and learned a more data-efficient RL algorithm than the one that generated its data.
Takeaway
An agent can learn from its own experience inside the context window if it sees its past attempts and outcomes; keep those histories.

PaLM-E: An Embodied Multimodal Language Model (our pick for Danny Driess's talk)

Driess, Xia, Sajjadi, Lynch, Chowdhery, Ichter, et al. · 2023

What
Feed images and robot state directly into a pre-trained language model as 'multimodal sentences', trained end to end for robot planning, visual question answering and captioning.
Result
The largest model, PaLM-E-562B, reached state-of-the-art on OK-VQA and kept its general language skills; joint training on internet-scale data helped robot tasks.
Takeaway
Reuse a big pre-trained model for a new input type rather than training a specialist from scratch.

π0: A Vision-Language-Action Flow Model for General Robot Control (our pick for Danny Driess's talk)

Black, Brown, Driess, Esmail, Equi, Finn, et al. (Physical Intelligence) · 2024

What
A flow-matching action model built on a pre-trained vision-language model, trained on data from many robot types (single-arm, dual-arm, mobile).
Result
Evaluated zero-shot after pre-training, on following language instructions, and on learning new skills by fine-tuning, on tasks such as laundry folding, table cleaning and box assembly. (We did not extract per-task numbers.)
Takeaway
The recipe from language agents carries over to robots: broad pre-training, then fine-tuning on high-quality task data.

The code, cell by cell

Everything below is exactly what runs in a11_guest_lectures.ipynb, in the same order. The notebook starts with the shared setup cell (shown in Module 00), then these cells.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 2
import json, re, time
from collections import Counter
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
import httpx
from lal.llm import ask
from lal.tasks import math_problems, extract_int
from lal.checks import check

PROBS = math_problems(6, seed=11, difficulty="hard")      # 3-5 step word problems with exact answers
print(PROBS[0]["question"], "->", PROBS[0]["answer"])

def acc(preds):
    return sum(p == q["answer"] for p, q in zip(preds, PROBS)) / len(PROBS)

def pmap(fn, xs):
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
        return list(ex.map(fn, xs))

1. Direct answer vs chain of thought (Denny Zhou, Lecture 15)

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al. 2022, Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models): let the model write its steps before the answer. With eight worked examples, a 540B model reached state-of-the-art on GSM8K grade-school math, beating a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier. Today's chat models are trained to reason this way, so a plain "think step by step" is usually enough. We compare that with forcing the answer right away.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 3
DIRECT = "{q}\nReply with only the final number, no words, no working. Format: ANSWER: <number>"
COT = "{q}\nThink step by step, showing each calculation. Then end with: ANSWER: <number>"

direct = pmap(lambda q: extract_int(ask(DIRECT.format(q=q["question"]), max_tokens=15)), PROBS)        # 6 calls
cot = pmap(lambda q: extract_int(ask(COT.format(q=q["question"]), max_tokens=500)), PROBS)             # 6 calls
print(f"direct: {acc(direct):.0%} | chain of thought: {acc(cot):.0%}")
for q, d, c in zip(PROBS, direct, cot):
    print(f"  truth {q['answer']:5} | direct {d} | cot {c}")

2. Self-consistency: sample several chains, take the majority

Self-consistency (Wang et al. 2022, Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models): sample several reasoning paths at a temperature above 0 and take the most common final answer. A hard problem has many paths to the right answer but the wrong answers tend to scatter. They report gains over single-chain CoT of +17.9% on GSM8K, +11.0% on SVAMP and +12.2% on AQuA. How often the samples agree is also a free confidence score.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 4
K = 5
def sample_answers(q):
    return [extract_int(ask(COT.format(q=q["question"]), temperature=0.7, max_tokens=500)) for _ in range(K)]

samples = pmap(sample_answers, PROBS)                                                                   # 30 calls
votes = [Counter(a for a in s if a is not None).most_common(1) for s in samples]
sc = [v[0][0] if v else None for v in votes]
agree = [v[0][1] / K if v else 0 for v in votes]
print(f"single CoT: {acc(cot):.0%} | self-consistency@{K}: {acc(sc):.0%}")
for q, s, a, v in zip(PROBS, samples, agree, sc):
    print(f"  truth {q['answer']:5} | samples {s} | vote {v} | agreement {a:.0%} | {'right' if v == q['answer'] else 'wrong'}")

Look at the agreement column: when most samples agree, the majority is usually right. That is how you decide, in production, which answers to send to a person for review.

3. Least-to-most: split the problem, then solve the parts in order

Least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al. 2022, Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models): first ask the model to break the problem into simpler sub-questions, then solve them in order, each answer feeding the next. CoT struggles when test problems are harder than the examples; least-to-most reached at least 99% on every split of the SCAN benchmark with 14 examples, versus 16% for CoT.

The paper solves each sub-question in its own call. To save calls we do it in two: one call to decompose, one to solve all sub-questions in order.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 5
def least_to_most(q):
    subs = ask(f"{q['question']}\n\nDo not solve it yet. List the simpler sub-questions, in order, that you need to answer to solve this. "
               "One per line, at most 5.", max_tokens=200)
    solved = ask(f"Problem: {q['question']}\n\nSub-questions:\n{subs}\n\nAnswer each sub-question in order, using earlier answers. "
                 "Then end with: ANSWER: <number>", max_tokens=600)
    return extract_int(solved), subs

l2m_out = pmap(least_to_most, PROBS)                                                                   # 12 calls
l2m = [a for a, _ in l2m_out]
print(f"least-to-most: {acc(l2m):.0%}")
print("example decomposition:\n" + l2m_out[0][1][:400])

4. CoT-decoding: reasoning without a prompt

CoT-decoding (Wang and Zhou 2024, Chain-of-Thought Reasoning Without Prompting) shows that a model often "knows" how to reason, but greedy decoding hides it. Give the plain format Q: ... A: with no instructions. At the first answer token, branch on the top-k alternatives (k = 10 in the paper), then continue each branch greedily. Branches that contain reasoning tend to end in an answer the model is more confident about. They measure confidence as Δ: the average gap between the top-1 and top-2 token probabilities over the answer's tokens. On GSM8K, Mistral-7B (pre-trained) went from 9.9% with greedy decoding to 25.1% with CoT-decoding.

We need the raw text-completion endpoint for this, so we call Ollama's own API (/api/generate with raw: true, which skips the chat template). If your Ollama returns token log-probabilities, we take the real top-k first tokens and compute Δ. If not, we approximate: sample several different first tokens at a high temperature, and use agreement between branches as the confidence instead of Δ.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 6
OLLAMA = "http://localhost:11434"

def generate(prompt, num_predict, temperature=0.0, seed=0, logprobs=False):
    body = {"model": MODEL, "prompt": prompt, "raw": True, "stream": False,
            "options": {"temperature": temperature, "num_predict": num_predict, "seed": seed, "stop": ["\nQ:"]}}
    if logprobs:
        body.update({"logprobs": True, "top_logprobs": 5})
    r = httpx.post(f"{OLLAMA}/api/generate", json=body, timeout=180)
    r.raise_for_status()
    return r.json()

def first_tokens(prompt, k=5):
    """Top-k first tokens: from log-probs if the server gives them, else by sampling."""
    try:
        lp = generate(prompt, 1, logprobs=True).get("logprobs") or []
        tops = [t.get("token", "") for t in (lp[0].get("top_logprobs") or [])] if lp else []
        if len(tops) >= 2:
            return tops[:k], True
    except Exception as e:
        print("log-probs not available:", type(e).__name__)
    seen = []
    for s in range(2 * k):                         # sample until we have k different first tokens (cheap 1-token calls)
        tok = generate(prompt, 1, temperature=1.2, seed=s).get("response", "")
        if tok not in seen:
            seen.append(tok)
        if len(seen) == k:
            break
    return seen, False

def delta(lp_list, text):
    """Average top-1 minus top-2 probability over the tokens of the last number in the text (the paper's Δ)."""
    import math
    nums = re.findall(r"-?\d[\d,]*", text)
    if not nums or not lp_list:
        return None
    target, gaps, built = nums[-1], [], ""
    for t in reversed(lp_list):                    # walk back from the end until we have covered the answer tokens
        tok = t.get("token", "")
        built = tok + built
        tops = sorted((math.exp(x.get("logprob", -99)) for x in (t.get("top_logprobs") or [])), reverse=True) + [0, 0]
        if any(ch.isdigit() for ch in tok):
            gaps.append(tops[0] - tops[1])
        if target in built and gaps:
            break
    return sum(gaps) / len(gaps) if gaps else None

def cot_decode(q, k=5):
    prompt = f"Q: {q['question']}\nA:"
    toks, have_lp = first_tokens(prompt, k)
    branches = []
    for tok in toks:
        out = generate(prompt + tok, 250, logprobs=have_lp)
        text = tok + out.get("response", "")
        branches.append({"first": tok, "answer": extract_int(text), "text": text,
                         "delta": delta(out.get("logprobs") or [], text) if have_lp else None})
    counts = Counter(b["answer"] for b in branches if b["answer"] is not None)
    for b in branches:
        b["agreement"] = counts.get(b["answer"], 0) / len(branches)
    # Pick the answer: by summed Δ if we have it (the paper's weighted aggregation), else by agreement.
    score = Counter()
    for b in branches:
        if b["answer"] is not None:
            score[b["answer"]] += b["delta"] if b["delta"] is not None else 1
    return branches, (score.most_common(1)[0][0] if score else None), have_lp

try:
    cd_results = []
    for q in PROBS[:1]:                                                                                 # about 20 short calls
        branches, pick, have_lp = cot_decode(q)
        greedy = branches[0]["answer"] if have_lp else extract_int(generate(f"Q: {q['question']}\nA:", 250).get("response", ""))
        cd_results.append((q["answer"], greedy, pick))
        print(f"\ntruth {q['answer']} | greedy {greedy} | CoT-decoding pick {pick} | real log-probs: {have_lp}")
        for b in branches:
            d = f"{b['delta']:.2f}" if b["delta"] is not None else "n/a"
            print(f"  first token {b['first']!r:10} -> answer {b['answer']} | Δ {d} | agreement {b['agreement']:.0%} | {b['text'][:70]!r}")
except Exception as e:
    cd_results = []
    print("Skipped CoT-decoding (needs Ollama's /api/generate):", type(e).__name__, e)

What to notice: some first tokens start the answer right away ("12"), others start a sentence ("First", "The") and lead into worked steps. Our model is instruction-tuned, so it often reasons anyway; the effect is biggest on pre-trained (base) models, which is what the paper studied. The paper also found instruction-tuned Mistral-7B still gained (31.2% to 38.2% on GSM8K).

5. The other talks, in short (readings are our picks)

Melvin Johnson (Google DeepMind), Lecture 9: "Evolution of Post-training from Chatbots to Agents." His personal site describes him as leading Gemini post-training. Post-training is everything after pre-training: instruction tuning, preference tuning, and reinforcement learning, which together turn a text predictor into a helpful chat model and now into an agent that plans and calls tools. Readings we chose: the Gemini 1.0 report (Gemini Team 2023, Gemini: A Family of Highly Capable Multimodal Models), where he is an author and which describes the post-training stages; and his best-known earlier paper (Johnson et al. 2017, Google's Multilingual Neural Machine Translation System), where one model handles many languages with only a target-language token added, and can translate between pairs it never saw together ("zero-shot translation"). The lesson that carries over: one general model plus the right training signal beats many special-purpose models.

Thang Luong (Google DeepMind), Lecture 16: "Towards AI Superhuman Reasoning: AlphaProof, AlphaGeometry and Gemini IMO Gold." - AlphaGeometry (Trinh et al. 2024, Solving olympiad geometry without human demonstrations, Nature): a language model proposes helpful extra constructions (a new point or line) and a symbolic engine does the rigorous deduction. Trained on 100 million synthetic theorems and proofs, with no human demonstrations. It solved 25 of 30 olympiad geometry problems; the previous best method solved 10, and an average gold medalist about 25.9. - AlphaProof + AlphaGeometry 2 (DeepMind blog, July 2024): reinforcement learning in the formal proof language Lean, on about a million problems auto-translated from natural language. Together they scored 28 of 42 at IMO 2024 (4 of 6 problems), silver-medal level, but some problems took up to three days. - Gemini Deep Think (DeepMind blog, July 2025): 35 of 42 at IMO 2025 (5 of 6 problems), gold-medal level, end to end in natural language within the 4.5-hour limit, using "parallel thinking" (exploring several solution paths at once) and new RL techniques. The arc: from a verifier-backed narrow system to a general model that reasons in plain language. A checkable reward (a proof checker, a unit test, our contract labels) is what made the RL work.

Misha Laskin (Reflection AI), Lecture 18: "Building Agentic Systems for Autonomy: Lessons and Open Questions." Laskin co-founded Reflection AI in March 2025 after DeepMind work on RL and Gemini; its stated bet is that solving autonomous coding leads toward broader superintelligence. Its first product, Asimov, reads code plus team documents and messages to answer how a codebase works (as reported in July 2025). Reading we chose: his earlier paper (Laskin et al. 2022, In-context Reinforcement Learning with Algorithm Distillation), which trains a transformer on the learning histories of an RL algorithm; the model then improves its policy in context, with no weight updates. That is a clean early version of this course's theme: an agent that gets better from its own experience.

Danny Driess (Physical Intelligence), Lecture 19: "Multimodal AI Agents in Robotics." Readings we chose: PaLM-E (Driess et al. 2023, PaLM-E: An Embodied Multimodal Language Model), which feeds images and robot state straight into a language model as "multimodal sentences"; the largest version (562B parameters) did robot planning and reached state-of-the-art on the OK-VQA visual question benchmark. And π0 (Black et al. 2024, π0: A Vision-Language-Action Flow Model for General Robot Control), co-authored by Driess: a vision-language model extended with a flow-matching "action expert" that outputs smooth robot motions, trained on many robot types, doing tasks like folding laundry and assembling boxes. The pattern from the rest of the course holds: start from a pre-trained model, add the new input and output types, and fine-tune for the task.

Lecture 20: Future research areas. No readings were listed; this paragraph is our synthesis of open problems that run through the course, not the lecture's content: (1) rewards for work that has no unit test (most business work); (2) long-horizon reliability, since METR's 80% horizons are several times shorter than its 50% ones; (3) memory that stays correct over months; (4) cheaper inference so that search, voting and self-checking are affordable; (5) evaluations that keep up with models (live benchmarks like DeepScholar-Bench).

Check yourself

What is the difference between self-consistency and CoT-decoding?

Self-consistency samples whole reasoning paths from a CoT prompt and votes; CoT-decoding uses no prompt, branches only on the first token's top-k alternatives, continues greedily, and picks by answer confidence.

Why does least-to-most help on problems harder than the examples?

Breaking the problem into easier sub-problems solved in order lets the model handle each piece at the difficulty it can manage, with earlier answers feeding later ones.

What changed between the 2024 IMO silver and the 2025 IMO gold?

2024 used formal Lean proofs and took up to three days (28/42); 2025 Gemini Deep Think worked end to end in natural language within 4.5 hours (35/42).

What made RL work in AlphaProof?

A formal checker (Lean) that verifies proofs exactly, giving a reliable reward.
