One arc through the talks

3/64
  • Reason step by step (chain of thought).
  • Sample several answers and vote (self-consistency).
  • Split hard problems into easy ones (least-to-most).

Part 2Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al. 2022)

The idea

5/64
  • Chain of thought (CoT): a series of short reasoning steps written before the answer.
  • Few-shot prompting: show the model a few worked examples, then the new question.
  • CoT prompting: each example is a triple ⟨input, chain of thought, output⟩.
  • No training or fine-tuning. One model checkpoint, many tasks.

Standard vs chain-of-thought prompt (Figure 1)

6/64
Standard prompting Chain-of-thought prompting
Example answer: "The answer is 11." Example answer: "Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11."
Cafeteria question → "The answer is 27." (wrong) Cafeteria question → "23 - 20 = 3 ... 3 + 6 = 9. The answer is 9." (right)

Why CoT helps (Section 2)

7/64
  • Breaks multi-step problems into steps, so more compute goes to harder problems.
  • Gives a readable window into how the model got its answer.
  • Works for any task people solve in language: math, commonsense, symbols.
  • Easy to use: just put worked examples in the prompt.

Setup (Section 3.1)

8/64
  • 5 math benchmarks: GSM8K, SVAMP, ASDiv, AQuA, MAWPS.
  • 8 hand-written CoT examples for all math sets except AQuA (4 examples).
  • Models: GPT-3 (350M to 175B), LaMDA (422M to 137B), PaLM (8B, 62B, 540B), UL2 20B, Codex.
  • Greedy decoding.

Three takeaways (Section 3.2)

10/64
  • CoT is an emergent ability of scale: it only helps at about 100B parameters.
    • Smaller models wrote fluent but illogical chains, scoring below standard prompting.
  • Bigger gains on harder problems: GSM8K more than doubled for the largest models.
    • On one-step problems (SingleOp), gains were negative or very small.
  • With CoT, large models match or beat fine-tuned task-specific models.

Are the chains correct? (Section 3.2)

11/64
  • LaMDA 137B, GSM8K, 50 correct answers: all chains correct except two that got lucky.
  • 50 wrong answers: 46% almost correct (calculator error, symbol mix-up, one missing step); 54% had major errors.
  • Scaling PaLM 62B → 540B fixed many missing-step and understanding errors.

Robustness (Section 3.4)

13/64
  • Three annotators wrote different chains; a concise style too; three sets sampled from GSM8K training data.
  • All beat standard prompting by a large margin (Figure 6). No special wording needed.
  • Also robust to example order and number of examples.

Beyond math (Sections 4–5)

14/64
  • Commonsense with PaLM 540B + CoT:
    • StrategyQA: 75.6% vs prior best 69.4%.
    • Sports understanding: 95.4% vs 84% for an unaided sports enthusiast.
    • CSQA: minimal gain.
  • Symbolic (last-letter concatenation, coin flip): CoT helps generalize to longer inputs than the examples (2 words → 3 or 4).

CoT's stated limits (Section 6)

15/64
  • Doesn't settle whether the network is really "reasoning".
  • Writing chains is cheap for a few examples, costly for fine-tuning data.
  • No guarantee the chain is correct.
  • Only works at large scale, which is costly to serve.

Part 3Self-consistency (Wang et al. 2022)

The idea

17/64
  • Replace greedy decoding in CoT with "sample and marginalize".
    1. Prompt with chain-of-thought examples.
    1. Sample many diverse reasoning paths (temperature above 0).
    1. Take the most common final answer.
  • Intuition: a hard problem has many paths to the right answer; wrong paths rarely agree.

Example (Figure 1)

18/64
Path Reasoning Answer
Greedy "3 + 4 = 7 eggs every day ... 7 * $2 = $14" $14 (wrong)
Sample 1 "16 - 3 - 4 = 9 eggs left ... $2 * 9 = $18" $18
Sample 2 "$2 * (16 - 4 - 3) = $26" $26
Sample 3 "16 - 3 = 13 ... 13 - 4 = 9 ... 9 * $2 = $18" $18
Majority vote $18 (right)

The formula (Section 2)

19/64
  • Majority vote: answer = argmax_a Σ_i 1(a_i = a) over m sampled outputs.
  • a_i: final answer parsed from path i ("The answer is X").
  • Weighted option: weight each path by P(r_i, a_i) = exp((1/K) · Σ_k log P(t_k | ...)).
    • K: number of tokens; this is the length-normalized probability.

Which vote works? (Table 1, PaLM-540B)

20/64
Aggregation GSM8K AQuA SVAMP
Greedy decode 56.5 35.8 79.0
Weighted sum (unnormalized) 59.9 38.2 76.2
Weighted sum (normalized) 74.1 48.0 86.8
Majority vote 74.4 48.3 86.6
  • Plain majority ≈ normalized weighting: the model sees all its paths as about equally likely.
  • That also means the model is poorly calibrated on its own.

Setup (Section 3.1)

21/64
  • Models: UL2-20B, GPT-3 (code-davinci-001 and -002), LaMDA-137B, PaLM-540B.
  • Same prompts as Wei et al.: 8 examples for math.
  • Sampling: T = 0.5, top-k 40 (UL2, LaMDA); T = 0.7, k = 40 (PaLM); T = 0.7 (GPT-3).
  • 40 sampled paths, averaged over 10 runs.

Compared with other methods (Section 3.4)

23/64
  • Sample-and-rank (pick the highest-probability sample): much smaller gain (Figure 3).
  • Beam search (UL2-20B, AQuA, 40 beams): 10.2% vs 26.9% for self-consistency (Table 6). Beams lack diversity.
  • Prompt ensembles on LaMDA-137B GSM8K (Table 7):
    • 3 prompt sets: 18.6; 40 prompt orders: 19.2; self-consistency: 27.7.

Extra findings (Section 3.3, 3.5)

24/64
  • Helps even where CoT hurts (Table 5): ANLI R1 standard 69.1, CoT 68.8, SC 78.5.
  • Robust to sampling settings (temperature, top-k, nucleus).
  • Imperfect prompts: greedy 17.1 → 14.9; SC brings it to 23.4 (Table 8).
  • Zero-shot CoT on PaLM-540B: 43.0 → 69.2 with SC (+26.2%).
  • More paths help; 5 or 10 get most of the gain (Figure 2).

Agreement as confidence (Figure 5)

25/64
  • Consistency: % of samples that agree with the final majority answer.
  • Highly correlated with accuracy on GSM8K.
  • Low consistency → the model "knows when it doesn't know".
  • Limits: costs more compute; models can still write wrong or nonsense reasoning.

Voting in code

26/64
samples = pmap(sample_answers, PROBS)
votes = [Counter(a for a in s if a is not None).most_common(1) for s in samples]
sc = [v[0][0] if v else None for v in votes]
agree = [v[0][1] / K if v else 0 for v in votes]
  • Line 1: K = 5 CoT samples per problem at temperature 0.7.
  • Line 2: count the final answers; find the most common.
  • Line 3: the majority answer.
  • Line 4: agreement = majority count / K.
  • Review gate (Solution 3): agreement below 60% → send to a person.
Part 4Least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al. 2022)

The problem: easy-to-hard generalization

27/64
  • CoT does poorly when test problems are harder than the prompt examples.
  • People can solve problems harder than any they've seen; models usually can't.
  • Name borrowed from education: a sequence of prompts that builds up a new skill.

Two stages (Section 2)

28/64
    1. Decomposition: examples show how to split a problem; then the new problem.
    1. Subproblem solving: examples of solving, plus earlier sub-questions and answers, plus the next sub-question.
  • The original problem is the last sub-question.
  • Both stages are few-shot prompts. No training.
  • Can merge into one pass, or combine with CoT and self-consistency.

Least-to-most vs CoT examples (Tables 2–3)

29/64
Least-to-most Chain of thought
"think, machine" outputs "ke". Same first example
"'think, machine' outputs 'ke'. The last letter of 'learning' is 'g' ... leads to 'keg'." "The last letter of 'think' is 'k' ... 'machine' is 'e' ... 'learning' is 'g' ... 'keg'."
Builds on the previous answer (base case + recursive step) Starts from scratch each time

Least-to-most's stated limits (Section 5)

33/64
  • Decomposition prompts don't carry across domains: a math one won't split "Did Aristotle use a laptop?"
  • Hard even within one domain: almost every GSM8K problem is solvable once given the right decomposition.
  • Great results on SCAN and letters come from easy decomposition.

Least-to-most in code

34/64
  • Stage 1: "Do not solve it yet. List the simpler sub-questions, in order... at most 5."
  • Stage 2: "Answer each sub-question in order, using earlier answers. Then end with: ANSWER: <number>"
  • Our notebook uses 2 calls (decompose, then solve all). The paper solves each sub-question in its own call.
Part 5CoT-decoding: reasoning without a prompt (Wang and Zhou 2024)

The question

35/64
  • Can a model reason with no prompt engineering at all?
  • Prompts carry human hints, so they hide what the model can do alone.
  • Input: plain Q: [question]\nA: (needed only so a base model answers instead of continuing the question).

Greedy vs top-k paths (Table 1, PaLM-2 Large)

36/64
Path Output Confidence Δ
Greedy (k=0) "$60.00" 0.029
k=1 "60" 0.058
k=2 "Kylar needs to pay $60 for 16 glasses." 0.058
k=9 "16 x 5 = 80 8 x 5 = 40 40 x 0.4 = 16 80 – 16 = 64 ... $64" 0.994
  • The right answer ($64) comes from the path that reasons; its Δ is far higher.
  • Majority of paths say 60, so self-consistency would pick wrong here.

The method

37/64
  • Branch on the top-k tokens at the first decoding step (k = 10 by default).
  • Continue each branch greedily.
  • Score each path by answer confidence Δ; pick the highest, or sum Δ per answer.
Greedy decoding CoT-decoding
Always pick the most likely next token Branch on the top-k first tokens
One path; often answers at once Continue each branch greedily
Hides reasoning the model can do Pick by answer confidence

The formula

38/64
  • Δ_k,answer = (1 / |answer|) · Σ_{x_t ∈ answer} [ p(x_t¹ | x_<t) − p(x_t² | x_<t) ]
  • x_t¹, x_t²: the top-1 and top-2 tokens at step t on path k.
  • Sum runs only over the answer's tokens (e.g. "6" and "0" for 60).
  • Big gap = model is sure. Similar to min-margin uncertainty.
  • Aggregated version: pick the answer with the largest Σ_k Δ_k,a.

39/64

10 paths, rank by length-normalized log-prob 51.0% 57.0%

Part 6Math olympiad: from formal to plain language

AlphaGeometry: the problem (Trinh et al. 2024)

45/64
  • Olympiad proofs need machine-checkable training data, which is scarce.
  • Geometry is worst: very few proofs in Lean; geometry-only languages can't express many human proofs.
  • Key hard step: auxiliary constructions (adding a new point or line). Infinite choices.

How AlphaGeometry works (Figure 1)

46/64
  • Neuro-symbolic: a language model plus a symbolic deduction engine.
    1. Symbolic engine deduces everything it can from the premises.
    1. Not solved? The language model proposes one auxiliary construction.
    1. Engine tries again. Loop until solved or out of budget (beam search over constructions).
  • Engine: DD (deductive database, Horn-clause rules) + AR (algebraic rules for angle, ratio and distance chasing).

Synthetic data, no human proofs (Figure 3)

47/64
  • Sample nearly 1 billion random premise sets; run the engine; trace back the minimal proof of each conclusion.
  • 100 million unique theorems and proofs; some over 200 steps.
  • Dependency difference: premises the conclusion doesn't need become auxiliary constructions.
    • Nearly 10 million such steps; about 9% of proofs.
  • Pre-train on all 100 million; fine-tune on the 9 million with constructions.
  • Model: 151 million parameter transformer, 12 layers, vocabulary of 757.

Part 7The other guest talks

Johnson: one model, many languages (2017)

54/64
  • Post-training: everything after pre-training (instruction tuning, preference tuning, RL). Johnson leads Gemini post-training (per his site); see the Gemini report.
  • His NMT paper: one translation model for many languages.
  • Only change: an artificial token for the target language, e.g. <2es> Hello, how are you?.
  • Same encoder, decoder, attention; shared 32,000 wordpiece vocabulary; no extra parameters.

Multilingual NMT results

55/64
  • WMT'14: matched state of the art English→French; beat it for English→German.
  • Many-to-one (Table 1): multilingual beat single models, e.g. French→English 35.50 → 36.77 BLEU.
  • Zero-shot Portuguese→Spanish (Table 5), never seen together in training:

NMT bridged through English no 30.91

Laskin: Algorithm Distillation (2022)

56/64
  • Policy distillation copies a fixed policy; it can't improve by trial and error.
  • Algorithm Distillation (AD): train a causal transformer on the full learning histories of an RL algorithm.
    • Loss: predict each action given all earlier (observation, action, reward) steps across episodes.
  • Because the policy in the data improves over time, the model must learn to improve.
  • At test time it improves in context, with no weight updates.

Algorithm Distillation results

57/64
  • Environments: adversarial bandit, Dark Room, Dark Key-to-Door, DMLab Watermaze (pixels).
  • Learns in context in all of them; expert distillation (expert data only) mostly fails.
  • Matches RL² (online meta-RL, 1 billion steps) on Dark tasks; within 13% on Watermaze.
  • More data-efficient than the A3C or DQN source algorithm.
  • Needs a context of 2–4 episodes; in-context RL starts at about one episode (Figure 7).
  • Limit: real tasks have long episodes, needing longer-context models.
  • Laskin later co-founded Reflection AI (March 2025), focused on autonomous coding agents.

Driess: PaLM-E (2023)

58/64
  • Inject images, robot state and scene features into a pre-trained language model as "multimodal sentences".
  • Trained end to end on robot planning, visual question answering and captioning.
  • PaLM-E-562B on OK-VQA: 66.1, above fine-tuned Flamingo (57.8, 32-shot) and PaLI (64.5) (Table 5).
  • Co-training on the full mixture more than doubled robot performance; robot tasks learned from 10–80 examples (Language-Table).
  • Bigger model forgets less language: NLG drop 87.3% for PaLM-E-12B vs 3.9% for 562B (relative).

Check your understanding

What is the difference between self-consistency and CoT-decoding?

Self-consistency samples whole reasoning paths from a CoT prompt and votes. CoT-decoding uses no prompt, branches only on the first token, continues greedily and picks by answer confidence.

Why did CoT not help small models in Wei et al.?

CoT is emergent with scale. Below about 100B parameters, models wrote fluent but illogical chains and did worse than standard prompting.

Why does least-to-most beat CoT on SCAN's length split?

It splits a long command into short ones and solves them in order, reusing earlier answers. CoT got 16.2%, least-to-most 99.7%.

In CoT-decoding, what is Δ?

The average gap between top-1 and top-2 token probabilities over the answer's tokens. Paths with reasoning tend to have a much higher Δ.

What changed between the 2024 IMO silver and the 2025 IMO gold?

2024 used formal Lean proofs and took up to three days (28/42). 2025 Gemini Deep Think worked end to end in natural language within 4.5 hours (35/42).

What made RL work in AlphaProof?

Lean, a formal checker that verifies proofs exactly, gave a reliable reward.

The papers

Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, Zhou · 2022

What
Prompting with a few worked examples that show intermediate reasoning steps, tested on three large models across arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning.
Result
A 540B model with eight chain-of-thought examples reached state-of-the-art accuracy on GSM8K, beating a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier.
Takeaway
Let the model show its work before the answer on multi-step tasks; then grade only the final answer with code.

Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Wang, Wei, Schuurmans, Le, Chi, Narang, Chowdhery, Zhou · 2022

What
Sample several diverse reasoning paths instead of one greedy path and take the most consistent final answer.
Result
Over chain-of-thought: GSM8K +17.9%, SVAMP +11.0%, AQuA +12.2%, StrategyQA +6.4%, ARC-challenge +3.9%.
Takeaway
Majority voting is a cheap accuracy boost, and the agreement rate is a free confidence score you can use to route low-agreement answers to a person.

Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Zhou, Schärli, Hou, Wei, Scales, Wang, Schuurmans, Cui, Bousquet, Le, Chi · 2022

What
Two stages: decompose a problem into simpler sub-problems, then solve them in order, feeding each answer into the next. Aimed at problems harder than the prompt examples.
Result
On SCAN with code-davinci-002 and 14 examples: at least 99% accuracy on every split, including the length split, vs 16% for chain-of-thought.
Takeaway
When tasks are longer than your examples, have the agent write a plan of sub-tasks first and execute them one by one.

Chain-of-Thought Reasoning Without Prompting (our pick for Denny Zhou's talk)

Wang, Zhou · 2024

What
Instead of greedy decoding, branch on the top-k alternatives for the first token (k = 10 by default), continue each greedily, and rank paths by answer confidence: the average gap between top-1 and top-2 token probabilities over the answer tokens.
Result
Mistral-7B (pre-trained) on GSM8K: greedy 9.9%, self-consistency without a CoT prompt 12.9%, CoT-decoding 25.1%. PaLM-2 Large: 34.8% greedy to 63.2%. Instruction-tuned Mistral-7B also improved (31.2% to 38.2%).
Takeaway
Reasoning paths often exist inside a model even when greedy decoding misses them; answer-token confidence is a useful signal for picking among candidates.

Solving olympiad geometry without human demonstrations (AlphaGeometry; our pick for Thang Luong's talk)

Trinh, Wu, Le, He, Luong · 2024

What
A neural language model proposes auxiliary constructions and a symbolic deduction engine does rigorous proofs, trained on 100 million synthetic theorems and proofs with no human demonstrations.
Result
Solved 25 of 30 recent olympiad geometry problems (IMO-AG-30); the previous best method solved 10, and an average human gold medalist about 25.9.
Takeaway
Pair a creative model with an exact checker, and generate your own training data when human data is scarce.

AI achieves silver-medal standard solving International Mathematical Olympiad problems (AlphaProof + AlphaGeometry 2 blog; our pick)

Google DeepMind · 2024

What
AlphaProof couples a pre-trained language model with AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning in the Lean proof language, trained on about a million problems auto-formalized by Gemini. AlphaGeometry 2 handled geometry.
Result
28 of 42 points at IMO 2024 (4 of 6 problems), silver level; some problems took up to three days. AlphaGeometry 2 solved 83% of IMO geometry problems from the past 25 years (predecessor 53%).

Reinforcement learning works best when a machine can check the answer exactly; formal verifiers are such checkers.

Advanced version of Gemini with Deep Think officially achieves gold-medal standard at the IMO (blog; our pick)

Google DeepMind · 2025

What
A Gemini model reasoning end to end in natural language, with 'parallel thinking' over several candidate solutions and new reinforcement-learning techniques for multi-step reasoning and proofs.
Result
35 of 42 points at IMO 2025 (5 of 6 problems), gold level, within the 4.5-hour limit, graded by IMO coordinators.

Parallel exploration plus RL on hard, checkable problems moved a general model past a specialized formal system in one year.

Google's Multilingual Neural Machine Translation System: Enabling Zero-Shot Translation (our pick for Melvin Johnson's talk)

Johnson, Schuster, Le, Krikun, Wu, Chen, Thorat, Viégas, Wattenberg, Corrado, Hughes, Dean · 2017

What
One translation model for many languages, with only a target-language token added to the input and no extra parameters. Background for the speaker, who now leads Gemini post-training (per his site); for his post-training work see the Gemini report (arXiv 2312.11805), where he is an author.

In-context Reinforcement Learning with Algorithm Distillation (our pick for Misha Laskin's talk)

Laskin, Wang, Oh, Parisotto, Spencer, Steigerwald, Strouse, Hansen, Filos, Brooks, Gazeau, Sahni, Singh, Mnih · 2022

def acc(preds):
    return sum(p == q["answer"] for p, q in zip(preds, PROBS)) / len(PROBS)

def pmap(fn, xs):
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
        return list(ex.map(fn, xs))

1. Direct answer vs chain of thought (Denny Zhou, Lecture 15)

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al. 2022, Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models): let the model write its steps before the answer. With eight worked examples, a 540B model reached state-of-the-art on GSM8K grade-school math, beating a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier. Today's chat models are trained to reason this way, so a plain "think step by step" is usually enough. We compare that with forcing the answer right away.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 3
DIRECT = "{q}\nReply with only the final number, no words, no working. Format: ANSWER: <number>"
COT = "{q}\nThink step by step, showing each calculation. Then end with: ANSWER: <number>"

direct = pmap(lambda q: extract_int(ask(DIRECT.format(q=q["question"]), max_tokens=15)), PROBS)        # 6 calls
cot = pmap(lambda q: extract_int(ask(COT.format(q=q["question"]), max_tokens=500)), PROBS)             # 6 calls
print(f"direct: {acc(direct):.0%} | chain of thought: {acc(cot):.0%}")
for q, d, c in zip(PROBS, direct, cot):
    print(f"  truth {q['answer']:5} | direct {d} | cot {c}")

2. Self-consistency: sample several chains, take the majority

Self-consistency (Wang et al. 2022, Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models): sample several reasoning paths at a temperature above 0 and take the most common final answer. A hard problem has many paths to the right answer but the wrong answers tend to scatter. They report gains over single-chain CoT of +17.9% on GSM8K, +11.0% on SVAMP and +12.2% on AQuA. How often the samples agree is also a free confidence score.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 4
K = 5
def sample_answers(q):
    return [extract_int(ask(COT.format(q=q["question"]), temperature=0.7, max_tokens=500)) for _ in range(K)]

samples = pmap(sample_answers, PROBS)                                                                   # 30 calls
votes = [Counter(a for a in s if a is not None).most_common(1) for s in samples]
sc = [v[0][0] if v else None for v in votes]
agree = [v[0][1] / K if v else 0 for v in votes]
print(f"single CoT: {acc(cot):.0%} | self-consistency@{K}: {acc(sc):.0%}")
for q, s, a, v in zip(PROBS, samples, agree, sc):
    print(f"  truth {q['answer']:5} | samples {s} | vote {v} | agreement {a:.0%} | {'right' if v == q['answer'] else 'wrong'}")

Look at the agreement column: when most samples agree, the majority is usually right. That is how you decide, in production, which answers to send to a person for review.

3. Least-to-most: split the problem, then solve the parts in order

Least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al. 2022, Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models): first ask the model to break the problem into simpler sub-questions, then solve them in order, each answer feeding the next. CoT struggles when test problems are harder than the examples; least-to-most reached at least 99% on every split of the SCAN benchmark with 14 examples, versus 16% for CoT.

The paper solves each sub-question in its own call. To save calls we do it in two: one call to decompose, one to solve all sub-questions in order.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 5
def least_to_most(q):
    subs = ask(f"{q['question']}\n\nDo not solve it yet. List the simpler sub-questions, in order, that you need to answer to solve this. "
               "One per line, at most 5.", max_tokens=200)
    solved = ask(f"Problem: {q['question']}\n\nSub-questions:\n{subs}\n\nAnswer each sub-question in order, using earlier answers. "
                 "Then end with: ANSWER: <number>", max_tokens=600)
    return extract_int(solved), subs

l2m_out = pmap(least_to_most, PROBS)                                                                   # 12 calls
l2m = [a for a, _ in l2m_out]
print(f"least-to-most: {acc(l2m):.0%}")
print("example decomposition:\n" + l2m_out[0][1][:400])

4. CoT-decoding: reasoning without a prompt

CoT-decoding (Wang and Zhou 2024, Chain-of-Thought Reasoning Without Prompting) shows that a model often "knows" how to reason, but greedy decoding hides it. Give the plain format Q: ... A: with no instructions. At the first answer token, branch on the top-k alternatives (k = 10 in the paper), then continue each branch greedily. Branches that contain reasoning tend to end in an answer the model is more confident about. They measure confidence as Δ: the average gap between the top-1 and top-2 token probabilities over the answer's tokens. On GSM8K, Mistral-7B (pre-trained) went from 9.9% with greedy decoding to 25.1% with CoT-decoding.

We need the raw text-completion endpoint for this, so we call Ollama's own API (/api/generate with raw: true, which skips the chat template). If your Ollama returns token log-probabilities, we take the real top-k first tokens and compute Δ. If not, we approximate: sample several different first tokens at a high temperature, and use agreement between branches as the confidence instead of Δ.

a11_guest_lectures.ipynb · cell 6
OLLAMA = "http://localhost:11434"

def generate(prompt, num_predict, temperature=0.0, seed=0, logprobs=False):
    body = {"model": MODEL, "prompt": prompt, "raw": True, "stream": False,
            "options": {"temperature": temperature, "num_predict": num_predict, "seed": seed, "stop": ["\nQ:"]}}
    if logprobs:
        body.update({"logprobs": True, "top_logprobs": 5})
    r = httpx.post(f"{OLLAMA}/api/generate", json=body, timeout=180)
    r.raise_for_status()
    return r.json()

def first_tokens(prompt, k=5):
    """Top-k first tokens: from log-probs if the server gives them, else by sampling."""
    try:
        lp = generate(prompt, 1, logprobs=True).get("logprobs") or []
        tops = [t.get("token", "") for t in (lp[0].get("top_logprobs") or [])] if lp else []
        if len(tops) >= 2:
            return tops[:k], True
    except Exception as e:
        print("log-probs not available:", type(e).__name__)
    seen = []
    for s in range(2 * k):                         # sample until we have k different first tokens (cheap 1-token calls)
        tok = generate(prompt, 1, temperature=1.2, seed=s).get("response", "")
        if tok not in seen:
            seen.append(tok)
        if len(seen) == k:
            break
    return seen, False

def delta(lp_list, text):
    """Average top-1 minus top-2 probability over the tokens of the last number in the text (the paper's Δ)."""
    import math
    nums = re.findall(r"-?\d[\d,]*", text)
    if not nums or not lp_list:
        return None
    target, gaps, built = nums[-1], [], ""
    for t in reversed(lp_list):                    # walk back from the end until we have covered the answer tokens
        tok = t.get("token", "")
        built = tok + built
        tops = sorted((math.exp(x.get("logprob", -99)) for x in (t.get("top_logprobs") or [])), reverse=True) + [0, 0]
        if any(ch.isdigit() for ch in tok):
            gaps.append(tops[0] - tops[1])
        if target in built and gaps:
            break
    return sum(gaps) / len(gaps) if gaps else None

def cot_decode(q, k=5):
    prompt = f"Q: {q['question']}\nA:"
    toks, have_lp = first_tokens(prompt, k)
    branches = []
    for tok in toks:
        out = generate(prompt + tok, 250, logprobs=have_lp)
        text = tok + out.get("response", "")
        branches.append({"first": tok, "answer": extract_int(text), "text": text,
                         "delta": delta(out.get("logprobs") or [], text) if have_lp else None})
    counts = Counter(b["answer"] for b in branches if b["answer"] is not None)
    for b in branches:
        b["agreement"] = counts.get(b["answer"], 0) / len(branches)
    # Pick the answer: by summed Δ if we have it (the paper's weighted aggregation), else by agreement.
    score = Counter()
    for b in branches:
        if b["answer"] is not None:
            score[b["answer"]] += b["delta"] if b["delta"] is not None else 1
    return branches, (score.most_common(1)[0][0] if score else None), have_lp

try:
    cd_results = []
    for q in PROBS[:1]:                                                                                 # about 20 short calls
        branches, pick, have_lp = cot_decode(q)
        greedy = branches[0]["answer"] if have_lp else extract_int(generate(f"Q: {q['question']}\nA:", 250).get("response", ""))
        cd_results.append((q["answer"], greedy, pick))
        print(f"\ntruth {q['answer']} | greedy {greedy} | CoT-decoding pick {pick} | real log-probs: {have_lp}")
        for b in branches:
            d = f"{b['delta']:.2f}" if b["delta"] is not None else "n/a"
            print(f"  first token {b['first']!r:10} -> answer {b['answer']} | Δ {d} | agreement {b['agreement']:.0%} | {b['text'][:70]!r}")
except Exception as e:
    cd_results = []
    print("Skipped CoT-decoding (needs Ollama's /api/generate):", type(e).__name__, e)

What to notice: some first tokens start the answer right away ("12"), others start a sentence ("First", "The") and lead into worked steps. Our model is instruction-tuned, so it often reasons anyway; the effect is biggest on pre-trained (base) models, which is what the paper studied. The paper also found instruction-tuned Mistral-7B still gained (31.2% to 38.2% on GSM8K).

Check yourself

What is the difference between self-consistency and CoT-decoding?

Self-consistency samples whole reasoning paths from a CoT prompt and votes; CoT-decoding uses no prompt, branches only on the first token's top-k alternatives, continues greedily, and picks by answer confidence.

Why does least-to-most help on problems harder than the examples?

Breaking the problem into easier sub-problems solved in order lets the model handle each piece at the difficulty it can manage, with earlier answers feeding later ones.

What changed between the 2024 IMO silver and the 2025 IMO gold?

2024 used formal Lean proofs and took up to three days (28/42); 2025 Gemini Deep Think worked end to end in natural language within 4.5 hours (35/42).

What made RL work in AlphaProof?

A formal checker (Lean) that verifies proofs exactly, giving a reliable reward.
