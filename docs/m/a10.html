Learning objective

1/56
  • Measure an agent in human terms: how long a task it can finish, whether an expert would prefer its work, and separate scores for coverage, retrieval and citations.
Part 1Why this lecture exists

A benchmark score is not a product claim

2/56
  • "78% on a benchmark" doesn't tell a customer what the agent can do.
  • Old benchmarks have three problems (METR, Section 1):
    • Artificial tasks, not paid work
    • Tasks picked because models fail them, which skews the human comparison
    • They saturate fast, so GPT-2 and Claude 3.7 can't sit on one scale
  • This lecture: three ways to report results in human terms.

Three questions, three papers

3/56
Question Paper Number
How long a task can it finish? METR (Kwa et al. 2025) 50% time horizon, in human minutes
Would an expert prefer its work? GDPval (Patwardhan et al. 2025) Win-or-tie rate vs an expert
Is its research report sound? DeepScholar-Bench (Patel et al. 2025) Separate coverage, retrieval, citation scores

What the three share

4/56
  • Ground truth comes from people: timed humans, expert deliverables, human-written sections.
  • Each reports more than one number: 50% and 80% horizons; speed with and without review; 7 metrics.
  • Each names its limits: tidy tasks, one-shot prompts, LLM judges.
Part 2METR: measuring the time horizon

The metric

5/56
  • X% time horizon: the length of tasks (in human time) a model finishes X% of the time.
  • 50% time horizon: the human time of tasks the model finishes half the time.
  • Human time: how long a skilled person with domain knowledge, but no task-specific context, takes.
  • Inspired by Item Response Theory (IRT): the psychometrics behind standardized tests.

The method in three steps (Figure 2)

6/56
    1. Build a task suite: 170 tasks from seconds to hours.
    1. Humans and agents both try the tasks.
    2. Record the time of successful humans.
    3. Record the success rate of each agent (model + scaffold).
    1. Fit a logistic curve per agent; read the 50% point; plot it against release date.

  • All tasks are scored automatically by code.

Human baselines (Section 2.2)

9/56
  • Over 800 baselines, 2,529 hours in total.
    • 558 baselines (286 successful) on HCAST and RE-Bench
    • 249 baselines (236 successful) on SWAA
  • 148 of 169 tasks have human baselines; 21 HCAST tasks use researcher estimates.
  • Baseliners: software, ML and cybersecurity professionals, about 5 years of experience on average.
  • Task difficulty = geometric mean time of successful baselines.

Running the agents (Section 2.3)

10/56
  • 12 frontier (and 4 near-frontier) models, released 2019 to 2025.
  • About 8 runs per agent and task.
  • Each task weighted by 1 / √(tasks in its family), so big families don't dominate.
  • Recent models finish about 50% of all tasks; earlier ones far fewer (Figure 3).
  • Success falls as human time rises: y = −0.07x + 0.66, R² 0.83 (Figure 3, right).

The formula (Section 3.1)

11/56
  • p_success(agent, task) = σ((log h_agent − log t_task) · β_agent)
  • σ: the logistic (S-shaped) function, from 0 to 1.
  • t_task: geometric mean time of successful humans on the task.
  • h_agent: learned; the task length where success is 50%. This is the 50% time horizon.
  • β_agent: learned slope; how fast success drops as tasks get longer.
  • Continuous scores are turned into pass/fail with a threshold tied to human performance.

Results: doubling every 7 months (Section 3.2, Figure 1)

12/56
  • Regress log(horizon) on release date.
  • 50% horizon doubled every 207 days; 95% CI 166–240 days.
  • GPT-2: about 2 seconds. o3: about 110 minutes (v4 abstract).
    • The first version's abstract gave Claude 3.7 Sonnet about 50 minutes.
  • o3 sits above the long-run trend (p = 0.006): 2024–2025 may be faster.
  • More confident in the slope than in any one model's horizon.

50% vs 80% horizon (Section 3.2.1)

13/56

The headline number 4–6x shorter

  • 95% horizons can't be measured with this data set.

Messy tasks (Appendix F.2)

16/56
  • 16 "messiness" factors: under-specified, no clear feedback, limited resources, real-time coordination...
  • Mean score of HCAST and RE-Bench tasks: 3.2/16; none above 8/16.
    • "Write a good research paper" would score 9–15/16.
  • Each extra point of messiness cuts success by roughly 8.1% (b = −0.081, R² 0.251).
  • But the improvement trend is similar on messy and clean tasks: sub-hour tasks gained 40 percentage points from Jan 2023 to May 2025 in both splits.

External checks (Section 4)

18/56
  • SWE-bench Verified: same exponential trend, faster doubling (about 70 days vs 143 days for the 2024 models).
    • Likely because its time labels underestimate how long contractors take on easy tasks.
  • Extrapolation: one-month (167 work hours) horizon between mid-2028 and mid-2031 (Figure 6); central estimate mid-2029.

METR's stated limits (Appendix B.2, Section 6)

19/56
  • All tasks are scored by code: less open-ended than real work.
  • No other agents to work with or against.
  • Lax resource limits; few tasks punish a single mistake.
  • Static environments.
  • Horizon is always relative to a domain, task mix and the baseliners' skill.
Part 3Our toy version of METR

Multi-step contract checks

20/56
  • A task with N steps: walk through N facts from a contract fact sheet, keep a running total.
    • "Add the notice days of msa_..." / "Subtract the renewal years of msa_..."
  • 15 tasks, 1 to 32 steps. Each run twice (METR: 8 times).
  • Assumed human time: 1 minute + 1.5 minutes per step (2.5 to 49 minutes).
    • Our guess. METR timed real people. The horizon is only as good as these times.

Fitting the curve in code

21/56
def horizon(w, level=0.5):
    a, b = w
    if b >= 0:                       # success does not fall with length: no horizon in this range
        return float("inf")
    return float(2 ** ((math.log(level / (1 - level)) - a) / b))
  • Model: P(success) = sigmoid(a + b · log2(minutes)), fit by Newton's method.
  • Line 2: a is the intercept, b the slope.
  • Lines 3–4: if success doesn't fall with length, there is no crossing point.
  • Line 5: solve a + b·x = log(level / (1 − level)); 2^x is the horizon in minutes.
  • level 0.8 gives the 80% horizon. A bootstrap over tasks gives a rough range.
Part 4GDPval: grading real deliverables

The idea

22/56
  • Measure economic value directly: can a model do the work people are paid for?
  • Usage and GDP statistics lag; capability tests lead.
  • Task = a request (often with reference files) + a deliverable (the work product).
  • Score: head-to-head comparison with an expert's deliverable, judged by other experts.

Choosing occupations (Section 2.1)

23/56
  • Sectors contributing over 5% of US GDP (Q2 2024): 9 sectors.
  • In each, the 5 occupations with the most total wages that are "predominantly digital".
    • Digital: GPT-4o labeled each O*NET task; occupation counts if at least 60% of its weighted tasks are digital.
  • 44 occupations, earning $3T a year together.

The 9 sectors (Table 1)

24/56
Sector % GDP Example occupations
Real estate and rental 13.8% Property managers, real estate agents
Government 11.3% Compliance officers, social workers
Manufacturing 10.0% Industrial engineers, buyers
Professional, scientific, technical 8.1% Software developers, lawyers, accountants
Health care 7.6% Registered nurses, health services managers
Finance and insurance 7.4% Financial managers, analysts

Who wrote the tasks (Section 2.2–2.4)

25/56
  • Experts with at least 4 years in their occupation; 14 years on average.
  • Passed a video interview, background check, training and quiz.
  • 1,320 tasks in the full set (at least 30 per occupation); 220 in the public gold subset (5 per occupation).
  • Each task: an average of 5 human reviews (minimum 3).
  • Task value = estimated hours × median hourly wage for the occupation.

What the tasks look like

26/56
  • Real files: CAD, photos, video, audio, slide decks, spreadsheets, support chats.
  • Up to 17 reference files per task in the gold subset, 38 in the full set.
  • Average 7 hours of expert work; some span weeks.
  • Gold subset (Table 3): mean 9.49 hours, mean value $398.46, max $4,114.20.
  • 67.7% of tasks need at least one reference file.

How grading works (Section 2.5)

27/56
  • Blind pairwise comparison: an occupation expert sees the request and two unlabeled deliverables, and ranks them.
  • Each comparison took over an hour on average.
  • Each model sampled 3 times per task, each sample graded by 3 experts: 9 comparisons per task per model.
  • Win rate: share judged better than the expert. Win-or-tie: better or as good.
  • Blinding isn't perfect: OpenAI outputs used em dashes; Claude used first person; Grok named itself.

Headline results (Section 3.1, Figure 5, Table 2)

28/56
  • Best on the gold subset: Claude Opus 4.1, 47.6% of deliverables win or tie vs the expert.
    • Strong on aesthetics: formatting, slide layout.
  • GPT-5 strongest on accuracy: following instructions, correct calculations.
  • Strict win rates (Table 2): GPT-4o 12.5%, o4-mini 29.1%, o3 35.2%, GPT-5 39.0%.
  • OpenAI models improved roughly linearly over time (Figure 6).

Why models lost (Section 3.3, Figure 8)

29/56
  • Claude, Grok and Gemini: mostly instruction-following failures.
  • GPT-5: mostly formatting errors; fewest instruction-following problems.
  • Gemini and Grok often promised but didn't deliver files, ignored reference data, or used the wrong format.
  • All models sometimes made up data or miscalculated.
  • Win rates highest on 0–2 hour tasks; they fall as tasks get longer (Figure 13).

Speed and cost: the definitions (Appendix A.2.1)

31/56
  • HT, HC: expert time and cost to do the task. Gold average: 404 minutes, $361.
  • RT, RC: expert time and cost to review a model deliverable. Average: 109 minutes, $86.
  • MT, MC: model time and cost (from API).
  • w: model win rate.

Speed and cost: three scenarios

32/56
  • Naive: HT / MT (ignores quality and review).
  • Try once, then fix: E[T] = MT + RT + (1 − w) · HT.
    • Model tries; expert reviews; with chance (1 − w) the expert does it themselves.
  • Try n times, then fix: resample and review up to n times before doing it yourself.
  • Same formulas for cost with MC, RC, HC.

What helped (Section 3.4)

34/56
  • More reasoning effort (low → medium → high) raised scores for o3 and GPT-5 (Figure 9a).
  • A checking prompt for GPT-5: render files as images, check layout, avoid odd unicode.
    • Black-square artifacts in PDFs: gone (had hit over half of PDFs).
    • Bad PowerPoint formatting: 86% → 64%.
    • Agents inspecting their own output: 15% → 97%.
    • Win rate: +5 percentage points (Figure 9b).
  • Best-of-4 sampling with a GPT-5 judge.
  • Less context hurt: prompts cut to 42% length made GPT-5 worse (Appendix A.2.7).

The automated grader (Appendix A.6)

35/56
  • Agreement: A = E[1 − |H − A|] with scores 0 (human wins), 0.5 (tie), 1 (model wins).
  • Grader vs expert: 65.7%. Expert vs expert: 70.8%.
  • Based on GPT-5-high; agrees less on outputs from strong OpenAI models (self-preference).
  • 12 of 220 gold tasks can't be auto-graded: need internet, non-Python code, missing fonts, or audio.

GDPval's stated limits (Section 5)

36/56
  • Only 44 occupations, 30 tasks each.
  • Computer work only; no manual labor, tacit knowledge, PII or people-to-people communication.
  • Tasks are fully specified and one-shot; real work means finding the context first.
  • Grader is a proxy, not a replacement for experts.
  • Expensive to build and run.

Order swap in code

39/56
first = grade_once(cid, model_text, expert_text)     # model is A
second = grade_once(cid, expert_text, model_text)    # model is B
if first == "A" and second == "B": return "win"
if first == "B" and second == "A": return "loss"
return "tie"
  • Lines 1–2: grade the same pair twice, positions swapped.
  • Line 3: model picked both times → win.
  • Line 4: expert picked both times → loss.
  • Line 5: any disagreement → tie. Small graders often pick the same position both times.
Part 6DeepScholar-Bench: scoring research reports

The problem

40/56
  • Research-synthesis systems search the web and write long, cited reports.
  • Existing tests fall short:
    • QA benchmarks want short, easy-to-check answers.
    • Expert-written datasets go stale and leak into training data.
  • Need a test that is realistic, fresh and scored on several axes.

The task and the data (Section 2)

41/56
  • Task: given a paper's title and abstract, retrieve sources and write its related-work section.
  • Data pipeline: scrape recent arXiv papers accepted at conferences; v1 only; must have a Related Work section and a .bib file.
  • DeepScholar-June-2025: April–June 2025, 18 arXiv CS domains, related work under 1,000 words.
  • 63 papers. Each human section cites 23 unique references on average; over 63% are on arXiv.
  • Live: new monthly sets planned. DeepScholar-Nov-2025: 200 queries, over 75 subject areas.

Seven metrics, three axes (Table 1)

42/56
Axis Metric What it measures
Knowledge synthesis Organization & coherency Pairwise LLM judge vs the human section
Nugget coverage Share of key facts (nuggets) from the human section present
Retrieval quality Relevance rate Average relevance of cited sources
Reference coverage Share of "important" human references found
Document importance How often the cited sources are cited elsewhere
Verifiability Citation precision Share of citations that support their sentence
Claim coverage Share of sentences fully supported by citations

The retrieval formulas (Section 3.2)

43/56
  • Relevance rate: RR(S) = (1 / 2|S|) · Σ Rel(s)
    • Rel(s): LLM judge scores each source 0, 1 or 2; divide by 2 to get 0–1.
  • Reference coverage: RC(S, E) = (1 / |E|) · Σ 1[s ∈ E]
    • E: references in the human section labeled "important" (can't be swapped for another).
  • Document importance: DI = min(median cites of S / median cites of human refs, 1).

Verifiability details (Section 3.3)

44/56
  • Citation precision: a citation counts if its source supports at least one claim in the sentence.
  • Claim coverage: a sentence scores 1 if its cited sources support every claim in it.
    • Relaxed: citations in a window of w sentences before or after count (w = 1 in the tables).
    • The query paper's own abstract counts as an implicit citation.
  • Both use LLM entailment checks.

DeepScholar-ref: the open baseline (Section 4, Figure 2)

45/56
    1. Write search queries, search, summarize results; repeat.
    1. Sem-filter: an LLM drops irrelevant sources.
    1. Sem-topk: an LLM ranks sources by relevance.
    1. Sem-agg: an LLM writes the section from what's left.
  • Built on LOTUS, a library of LLM "semantic operators".

Part 8How this applies to us

A claim the firm can check

52/56
  • Say "finishes X-minute contract checks Y% of the time", not "78% accurate".
  • Time real lawyers and paralegals on real review tasks; don't guess.
  • Time the right people: a partner who wrote the playbook is faster than a new associate (METR's 5–18x gap).

Count the review

53/56
  • Every agent output at a firm gets reviewed. That time counts.
  • Use GDPval's formula: model time + review time + (1 − win rate) × lawyer time.
  • A local model that wins 30% of comparisons may save little; a checking prompt and best-of-N can raise it.

Grade the deliverable, pairwise

54/56
  • Compare the agent's client email with a senior lawyer's, blind, both orders.
  • Use the local LLM grader for fast iteration; keep a lawyer spot-check, since graders agree with experts less than experts agree with each other.

Score citations separately

55/56
  • Contract summaries cite clauses. Check each cited clause supports its sentence.
  • Report coverage (did it find every risky clause?) apart from precision (are the cites right?).
  • All of this runs on the firm's own machine: Ollama model, local contracts, code metrics.
Part 9Wrap-up

Common mistakes

56/56
  • Guessing human times → time real people; the horizon depends on them.
  • Quoting the 50% horizon → quote 80% or more, and plan review around it.
  • Claiming "90x faster" → count review and redo time; GDPval's GPT-5 gain fell to 1.12x.
  • One-order LLM grader → swap positions; count disagreement as a tie.
  • Rewarding copied text → word-overlap metrics can be gamed by pasting sources; keep a judge for synthesis.
  • Fitting a curve when every run passed or failed → no crossing point; make longer or shorter tasks.

Check your understanding

How does METR turn success rates into a time horizon?

For each model, fit a logistic curve of success against log human time, then find the task length where it crosses 50%.

Why is the 80% horizon shorter than the 50% horizon?

Success falls as tasks get longer, so tasks must be shorter to succeed 80% of the time. METR found 4–6x shorter.

Why did GDPval's speed and cost advantage shrink?

An expert must review the model's output and fix or redo it when it is wrong. With that counted, GPT-5 went from 90x to 1.12x faster.

How well does GDPval's automated grader agree with experts?

65.7%, vs 70.8% between two experts. Useful for iteration, not a full replacement.

Name DeepScholar-Bench's three axes.

Knowledge synthesis, retrieval quality and verifiability.

What did the oracle-retrieval ablation show?

With perfect sources, retrieval and verifiability scores nearly max out, but nugget coverage stays near .5. Synthesis is still hard.

A summary cites a distractor source for a true sentence. Which of our metrics drops?

Retrieval precision (a non-relevant source was cited), and probably citation precision (the source doesn't support the sentence).

CS329A Lecture 17 (Nov 17)

Benchmark percentages do not tell a customer what an agent can do. Measure agents in human terms: the length of task (in human minutes) they finish half the time, whether an expert would prefer their work product over another expert's, and, for research reports, separate scores for coverage, retrieval and citations.

Key terms

Time horizon (50%)
The human time length of tasks that a model completes with 50% success, read from a fitted logistic curve.Source: Kwa et al. 2025
80% time horizon
The same, at 80% success; much shorter, and closer to what business use needs.Source: Kwa et al. 2025
Logistic curve
An S-shaped curve from 0 to 1; here, chance of success against log of task length.Source: general
Human baseline
A timed attempt by a skilled person; the task's difficulty is the geometric mean of successful times.Source: Kwa et al. 2025
Deliverable
The actual work product (memo, spreadsheet, slides) a task asks for, graded as a whole.Source: Patwardhan et al. 2025
Win-or-tie rate
Share of pairwise comparisons where the model's deliverable was judged as good as or better than the expert's.Source: Patwardhan et al. 2025
Order swap
Grading each pair twice with positions swapped, and treating a flip as a tie, to cancel position bias.Source: general
Nugget coverage
Share of key facts from the human-written reference that appear in the generated report.Source: Patel et al. 2025
Reference coverage
Share of the reference's important citations that the system found.Source: Patel et al. 2025
Citation precision
Share of citations whose source actually supports the sentence they are attached to.Source: Patel et al. 2025
Claim coverage
Share of sentences whose claims are all backed by cited sources.Source: Patel et al. 2025
Live benchmark
A benchmark refreshed with new items over time, so models cannot have trained on it.Source: Patel et al. 2025

The papers

Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks

Kwa, West, Becker, Deng, et al. (METR) · 2025

What
Timed skilled humans (over 800 baselines, 2,529 hours) on 170 tasks: 97 from HCAST, 7 from RE-Bench and 66 new short software tasks. For each model, fit a logistic curve of success against log human time (task difficulty = geometric mean of successful human times; tasks weighted by inverse square root of family size; 8 runs per agent and task) and read off the 50% time horizon.
Result
Frontier 50% horizons doubled about every 7 months since 2019 (207 days, 95% CI 166-240), possibly faster in 2024. Claude 3.7 Sonnet was about 50 minutes; GPT-2 about 2 seconds; a later revision reports o3 at about 110 minutes. 80% horizons were roughly 4-6x shorter than 50% horizons. Gains came mainly from reliability, recovering from mistakes, logical reasoning and tool use.
Takeaway
Describe your agent to customers as 'finishes X-minute tasks Y% of the time', and quote the high-reliability horizon (80% or more), not the 50% one. Build your task set across a range of lengths and time real people.

GDPval: Evaluating AI Model Performance on Real-World Economically Valuable Tasks

Patwardhan, Dias, Proehl, Kim, Wang, Watkins, et al. (OpenAI) · 2025

What
1,320 tasks across 44 occupations in the 9 sectors contributing most to US GDP, built from the work of professionals with 14 years of experience on average. Each task asks for a real deliverable (documents, slides, spreadsheets). Occupation experts compare model and human deliverables blind and pairwise; a 220-task gold subset and an automated grader are public.
Result
On the gold subset, Claude Opus 4.1 was best, with 47.6% of deliverables rated as wins or ties against the expert. Strict win rates: GPT-4o 12.5%, o4-mini 29.1%, o3 35.2%, GPT-5 39.0%. Naively GPT-5 was about 90x faster and 474x cheaper than experts, but with expert review and fixing ('try once, then fix') the gain shrank to about 1.12x faster and 1.18x cheaper. The automated grader agreed with experts 65.7% of the time vs 70.8% human-human agreement. More reasoning effort, more task context and more scaffolding all helped.
Takeaway
Grade agents on the actual work product against an expert reference, pairwise and blind, and include review time when you claim savings. An LLM grader is useful for iteration but is not as reliable as an expert.

DeepScholar-Bench: A Live Benchmark and Automated Evaluation for Generative Research Synthesis

Patel, Arabzadeh, Gupta, Sundar, Stoica, Zaharia, Guestrin · 2025

What
A live benchmark: queries and human-written related-work sections are drawn from recent arXiv papers, so it stays fresh and hard to contaminate. Systems must research and write a cited related-work section. Automatic metrics on three axes: knowledge synthesis (organization via pairwise LLM judge; nugget coverage), retrieval quality (relevance rate; reference coverage; document importance) and verifiability (citation precision; claim coverage). Also releases DeepScholar-ref, an open reference pipeline on LOTUS.
Result
The first set has 63 papers from 18 arXiv domains. No system exceeded a geometric mean of 31% across metrics; OpenAI DeepResearch scored 0.309 but only 0.138 claim coverage and 0.187 reference coverage. A Claude-based search agent had the highest verifiability (citation precision 0.701, claim coverage 0.760). DeepScholar-ref reached 0.285 at lower cost.
Takeaway
Score research and summary agents with separate metrics for coverage, retrieval and citation support. One overall score hides which part failed, and the best system on one axis is often weak on another.

1. METR's time horizon, on a toy

METR (Kwa et al. 2025, Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks) timed skilled people on 170 software tasks, from seconds to many hours. For each model they fit a logistic curve: chance of success against the log of the human time. The 50% time horizon is the human task length where that curve crosses 50%. They report it doubled about every 7 months since 2019 (Claude 3.7 Sonnet: about 50 minutes in the abstract).

Our toy: multi-step contract checks. A task with N steps asks the agent to walk through N facts from a contract fact sheet, keeping a running total. Each extra step is one more lookup and one more chance to slip. We assume a person needs 1 minute to set up plus 1.5 minutes per step. That is our made-up estimate; METR timed real people.

FACTS = {c: LAB[c]["terms"] for c in ids()}
SHEET = "id | monthly fee ($) | notice days | renewal years | liability cap (months) | governing law\n" + "\n".join(
    f"{c} | {t['monthly_fee']} | {t['notice_days']} | {t['renewal_years']} | {t['liability_cap_months'] if t['liability_cap_months'] else 'none'} | {t['governing_law']}"
    for c, t in FACTS.items())
CAPPED = [c for c, t in FACTS.items() if t["liability_cap_months"]]

def make_task(n_steps, seed):
    """A chain of n_steps lookups with an exact answer."""
    rng = random.Random(f"metr-toy-{n_steps}-{seed}")
    c0 = rng.choice(ids())
    total = FACTS[c0]["monthly_fee"] // 100
    steps = [f"Start with the monthly fee of {c0} divided by 100."]
    for _ in range(n_steps - 1):
        kind = rng.randrange(4)
        if kind == 0:
            c = rng.choice(ids()); total += FACTS[c]["notice_days"]
            steps.append(f"Add the notice days of {c}.")
        elif kind == 1:
            c = rng.choice(ids()); total -= FACTS[c]["renewal_years"]
            steps.append(f"Subtract the renewal years of {c}.")
        elif kind == 2:
            c = rng.choice(CAPPED); total += FACTS[c]["liability_cap_months"]
            steps.append(f"Add the liability cap in months of {c}.")
        else:
            c = rng.choice(ids()); ok = FACTS[c]["governing_law"] in PB["allowed_law"]
            total += 10 if ok else -10
            steps.append(f"Add 10 if the governing law of {c} is California or Delaware; otherwise subtract 10.")
    return {"n": n_steps, "minutes": 1 + 1.5 * n_steps, "steps": steps, "answer": total}

STEP_COUNTS = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32]     # 15 tasks, 2.5 to 49 "human minutes"
TASKS = [make_task(n, seed=0) for n in STEP_COUNTS]
print(TASKS[2])

def run_task(task, temperature=0.5):
    prompt = (f"Contract fact sheet:\n{SHEET}\n\nDo these steps in order. Write the running total after each step, "
              "then end with 'ANSWER: <number>'.\n" + "\n".join(f"{i+1}. {s}" for i, s in enumerate(task["steps"])))
    return extract_int(ask(prompt, temperature=temperature, max_tokens=1500)) == task["answer"]

Fit the logistic curve

Model: P(success) = sigmoid(a + b · log2(minutes)). We fit a and b by maximum likelihood with Newton's method (a few exact steps instead of many small ones), plus a tiny penalty so the fit stays finite when the data separate perfectly. The 50% horizon is where a + b·x = 0, so x = -a/b and horizon = 2^x minutes. The 80% horizon solves a + b·x = log(0.8/0.2).

a10_evals_long_horizon.ipynb · cell 5
def fit_logistic(minutes, success, l2=1e-2, iters=50):
    x = np.log2(np.asarray(minutes, float)); y = np.asarray(success, float)
    X = np.stack([np.ones_like(x), x], 1)
    w = np.zeros(2)
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w)))
        grad = X.T @ (p - y) + l2 * np.array([0, w[1]])
        H = X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]) + l2 * np.diag([1e-6, 1.0])
        w = w - np.linalg.solve(H, grad)
    return w

def horizon(w, level=0.5):
    a, b = w
    if b >= 0:                       # success does not fall with length: no horizon in this range
        return float("inf")
    return float(2 ** ((math.log(level / (1 - level)) - a) / b))

# Test the fit on fake data with a known answer: true 50% horizon = 16 minutes.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fake_m = np.repeat(2 ** np.linspace(0, 7, 15), 40)
fake_y = (rng.random(fake_m.size) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-1.2 * (np.log2(fake_m) - 4))))).astype(float)
w_fake = fit_logistic(fake_m, fake_y)
print(f"fake data: recovered 50% horizon {horizon(w_fake):.1f} min (true 16.0)")

w = fit_logistic(minutes, success)
h50, h80 = horizon(w, 0.5), horizon(w, 0.8)
print(f"our model: slope {w[1]:.2f}, 50% horizon {h50:.1f} min, 80% horizon {h80:.1f} min")

# Bootstrap over tasks for a rough interval (METR resampled families, tasks and runs).
task_idx = np.repeat(np.arange(len(TASKS)), N_RUNS)
boots = []
for _ in range(500):
    pick = rng.integers(0, len(TASKS), len(TASKS))
    mask = np.concatenate([np.where(task_idx == k)[0] for k in pick])
    boots.append(horizon(fit_logistic(minutes[mask], success[mask])))
finite = np.array([b for b in boots if np.isfinite(b)])
if finite.size:
    print(f"bootstrap 50% horizon: median {np.median(finite):.1f} min, 10th-90th percentile {np.percentile(finite, 10):.1f}-{np.percentile(finite, 90):.1f}")

How to read this. If the 50% horizon is, say, 12 minutes, the model finishes about half of tasks that would take a person 12 minutes, and fewer of the longer ones. The 80% horizon is always shorter: METR found 80% horizons about 4-6x shorter than 50% ones. For a business, the 80% (or higher) number is the one that matters, since a review step must catch every miss. Three caveats from the paper apply here too: the human times drive everything (ours are guesses); tasks within one family are related (METR down-weighted large families); and success on tidy tasks may not carry over to messy real work.

If the horizon prints as inf or is beyond 49 minutes, the model never dropped below 50% in our range: make longer tasks. If every run failed, make shorter ones.

2. GDPval: would an expert prefer this deliverable?

GDPval (Patwardhan et al. 2025, GDPval: Evaluating AI Model Performance on Real-World Economically Valuable Tasks) has 1,320 tasks across 44 occupations, written by professionals with about 14 years of experience on average. Each task asks for a real work product (a memo, a spreadsheet, a slide deck). Expert graders compare the model's deliverable against a human expert's, blind and pairwise, and the score is the win-or-tie rate. The best model on their gold set, Claude Opus 4.1, had 47.6% of deliverables rated as good as or better than the expert's. Their experimental automatic grader agreed with human experts 65.7% of the time, while human experts agreed with each other 70.8% of the time.

Our version: three client emails about contracts that break the playbook. We write the expert reference from the labels. An LLM grader compares the model's email with the expert's, in both orders.

TASK_CIDS = ["msa_08", "msa_10", "msa_17"]

    "msa_10": "Subject: msa_10 - governing law\n\nClause 10.1 chooses Florida law. Our playbook requires California or Delaware law. "
              "Recommendation: change 10.1 to Delaware (or California) law. The liability cap (12 months), one-year renewals and 30-day notice all meet the playbook.",
    "msa_17": "Subject: msa_17 - renewal and notice terms\n\nClause 4.1 has two problems. It renews automatically for five-year periods; the playbook allows one year at most. "
              "It also needs only 15 days' notice of non-renewal; the playbook requires at least 30 days. Recommendation: revise 4.1 to one-year renewals with at least 30 days' notice.",
}
TASK = ("Write a short email (under 120 words) to the client's general counsel about contract {cid}. Say which playbook rules it breaks, "
        "cite the clause numbers, and recommend a specific fix.\n\nPlaybook rules:\n{rules}\n\nContract:\n{doc}")

def deliverable(cid):
    return ask(TASK.format(cid=cid, rules=json.dumps(RULE_TEXT, indent=1), doc=contract(cid)), max_tokens=300)

GRADER = """You are a senior contracts lawyer grading two work products for the same task. You cannot see who wrote them.
Task: {task}
Playbook rules: {rules}
Contract:
{doc}

Email A:
{a}

Email B:
{b}

Judge correctness first (right rules, right clause numbers, workable fix), then clarity. Length is not quality.
Reply with one word: A, B, or TIE."""

def grade_once(cid, a, b):
    r = ask(GRADER.format(task=TASK.split("\n")[0].format(cid=cid), rules=json.dumps(RULE_TEXT), doc=contract(cid), a=a, b=b), max_tokens=5)
    m = re.search(r"\b(A|B|TIE)\b", r.upper())
    return m.group(1) if m else "TIE"

def pairwise(cid, model_text, expert_text):
    """Model vs expert, graded twice with the order swapped. Disagreement after the swap counts as a tie."""
    first = grade_once(cid, model_text, expert_text)     # model is A
    second = grade_once(cid, expert_text, model_text)    # model is B
    if first == "A" and second == "B": return "win"
    if first == "B" and second == "A": return "loss"
    return "tie"

with ThreadPoolExecutor(2) as ex:
    drafts = dict(zip(TASK_CIDS, ex.map(deliverable, TASK_CIDS)))         # 3 calls
verdicts = {c: pairwise(c, drafts[c], EXPERT[c]) for c in TASK_CIDS}      # 6 calls
for c in TASK_CIDS:
    print(f"--- {c}: {verdicts[c]}\n{drafts[c][:300]}\n")
win_or_tie = sum(v in ("win", "tie") for v in verdicts.values()) / len(verdicts)
print(f"win-or-tie rate vs expert: {win_or_tie:.0%}  ({verdicts})")

# Sanity check of the grader: a confidently wrong email should lose to the expert.
WRONG = "Subject: msa_17 looks good\n\nI reviewed msa_17. It meets every playbook rule, so the client can sign it as is."
sanity = pairwise("msa_17", WRONG, EXPERT["msa_17"])                    # 2 calls
print("wrong email vs expert:", sanity)

What to notice: the swap matters. Small graders often pick the same position both times, and we count that as a tie rather than trusting it. GDPval also found that review time eats most of the speed gain: for GPT-5, the raw ratio was about 90x faster than an expert, but once an expert reviews the output and redoes it when needed ("try once, then fix"), the gain was about 1.12x faster and 1.18x cheaper. Report both numbers to a customer.

3. DeepScholar-Bench: score a research summary automatically

DeepScholar-Bench (Patel et al. 2025, DeepScholar-Bench: A Live Benchmark and Automated Evaluation for Generative Research Synthesis) asks systems to write the related-work section of a recent arXiv paper, and scores three things: knowledge synthesis (e.g. nugget coverage: how many key facts from the human-written section appear), retrieval quality (e.g. reference coverage: how many important references were found) and verifiability (citation precision: does each cited source support its sentence; claim coverage: is every sentence backed by a citation). No system passed a geometric mean of 31% across their metrics.

Our version uses code only, so it is cheap and repeatable. We give the model six sources: three on agent evaluation (relevant) and three on memory and serving (distractors). It writes a short cited summary about evaluating agents.

One simplification: the paper uses an LLM judge to decide whether a source supports a sentence. We use a word-overlap rule instead (a cited source "supports" a sentence if they share at least 3 content words). It is crude but deterministic.

a10_evals_long_horizon.ipynb · cell 7
SOURCES = {
    "S1": "METR measures AI ability to complete long tasks with the 50% task-completion time horizon: the length of tasks, timed on skilled humans, that a model completes with 50% success. The horizon has doubled about every seven months since 2019.",
    "S2": "GDPval evaluates models on real-world economically valuable tasks from 44 occupations, written by industry experts. Experts compare model deliverables with expert deliverables in blind pairwise grading and report win or tie rates.",
    "S3": "DeepScholar-Bench is a live benchmark for generative research synthesis. It scores generated related-work sections on knowledge synthesis, retrieval quality and verifiability, and no system exceeds a geometric mean of 31 percent.",
    "S4": "MemGPT manages memory tiers for language models like an operating system, paging data between main context and archival storage through function calls.",
    "S5": "CacheBlend reuses precomputed KV caches of retrieved text chunks and recomputes a small subset of tokens, reducing time to first token.",
    "S6": "Cartridges train a small KV cache offline for a corpus using self-study and context distillation, cutting serving memory.",
}
GOLD = {"S1", "S2", "S3"}
NUGGETS = {   # key facts a good summary should contain: (description, regex)
    "50% time horizon": r"50\s*%|half|time horizon",
    "doubling about every 7 months": r"(7|seven)\s*months|doubl",
    "44 occupations / real work": r"44|occupation",
    "blind pairwise expert grading": r"pairwise|blind|win",
    "three axes: synthesis, retrieval, verifiability": r"verifiab|retrieval quality|synthesis",
    "benchmarks far from saturated (31%)": r"31|saturat",
}
STOP = set("the a an of and or to for in on with by is are that this it as from at be its their they about each than model models".split())

def content_words(s):
    return {w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9%]+", s.lower()) if w not in STOP and len(w) > 2}

def split_sentences(text):
    text = re.sub(r"([.!?])\s*((?:\[[^\]]+\]\s*)+)", lambda m: " " + m.group(2).strip() + m.group(1) + " ", text)   # "x. [S1]" -> "x [S1]."
    return [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text.strip()) if len(s.strip()) > 3]

def citations(sentence):
    return {f"S{n}" for group in re.findall(r"\[([^\]]+)\]", sentence) for n in re.findall(r"S\s*(\d+)", group)}

def scholar_metrics(text, sources=SOURCES, gold=GOLD, nuggets=NUGGETS):
    sents = split_sentences(text)
    cited = set().union(*[citations(s) for s in sents]) & set(sources) if sents else set()
    pairs = [(s, c) for s in sents for c in citations(s) if c in sources]
    supported = [len(content_words(re.sub(r"\[[^\]]+\]", "", s)) & content_words(sources[c])) >= 3 for s, c in pairs]
    sent_ok = [any(len(content_words(re.sub(r"\[[^\]]+\]", "", s)) & content_words(sources[c])) >= 3 for c in citations(s) if c in sources) for s in sents]
    m = {
        "nugget_coverage": sum(bool(re.search(p, text, re.I)) for p in nuggets.values()) / len(nuggets),
        "reference_coverage": len(cited & gold) / len(gold),
        "retrieval_precision": len(cited & gold) / len(cited) if cited else 0.0,
        "citation_precision": sum(supported) / len(supported) if supported else 0.0,
        "claim_coverage": sum(sent_ok) / len(sent_ok) if sent_ok else 0.0,
    }
    vals = list(m.values())
    m["geometric_mean"] = float(np.exp(np.mean(np.log(np.maximum(vals, 1e-3)))))
    return m

ORACLE = ("METR reports a 50% task-completion time horizon, the length of human-timed tasks a model completes with 50% success, which has doubled about every seven months since 2019 [S1]. "
          "GDPval evaluates real-world economically valuable tasks from 44 occupations, using blind pairwise grading by experts against expert deliverables [S2]. "
          "DeepScholar-Bench scores generated related-work sections on knowledge synthesis, retrieval quality and verifiability, and no system exceeds a geometric mean of 31 percent [S3].")
BAD = ("Agents are evaluated in many ways. Memory systems page data between main context and archival storage [S1]. "
       "Caching reduces time to first token [S5]. Benchmarks are important.")
for name, txt in [("oracle", ORACLE), ("bad", BAD)]:
    print(f"{name:7}", {k: round(v, 2) for k, v in scholar_metrics(txt).items()})

Traps
  • Guessing human times. The time horizon is only as good as the human baselines; our toy's made-up 1.5 minutes per step is exactly what METR avoided by timing people.
  • Quoting the 50% horizon to a customer. Half of tasks failing is not a product; quote the 80% or 95% horizon and design review around it.
  • Claiming '90x faster' without review time. GDPval's speed-up nearly vanished once expert review and fixes were counted.
  • Trusting a single-order LLM grader. Small graders often pick the same position both times; swap and count disagreement as a tie.
  • Rewarding long or copied text. Word-overlap metrics can be gamed by pasting sources; keep an LLM or human judge for organization and synthesis.
  • Fitting a curve when every run succeeded or every run failed. There is no crossing point in your range; make longer or shorter tasks.

Check yourself

How does METR turn success rates into a time horizon?

Fit a logistic curve of success against log human time per model, then find the task length where the curve crosses 50%.

Why is the 80% horizon shorter than the 50% horizon?

Success falls as tasks get longer, so to succeed 80% of the time the tasks must be shorter; METR found 4-6x shorter.

What does GDPval compare, and how?

The model's deliverable vs a human expert's for the same task, judged blind and pairwise by occupation experts; the score is the win-or-tie rate.

Why did GDPval's speed and cost advantage shrink?

Because an expert must review the model's output and fix or redo it when it is wrong; that review time counts.

Name the three axes DeepScholar-Bench scores.

Knowledge synthesis, retrieval quality and verifiability.

A summary cites a distractor source for a true sentence. Which of our metrics drops?

Retrieval precision (a non-relevant source was cited) and probably citation precision (the source does not support the sentence).
